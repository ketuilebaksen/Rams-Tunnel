Part one: what actually happened

Let’s go back to the Arizona game.

The Rams were already without Steve Avila, who’s dealing with a hamstring injury. Then, during the game, Coleman Shelton hurt his hamstring. Dylan McMahon came in to replace him. And then McMahon hurt his knee.

By the start of the second half, both were ruled out. Beaux Limmer, who started the game at left guard, slid over to center. Bill Murray came in at left guard. And David Quessenberry even played some snaps as an extra tight end.

McVay said after the game: “We needed all eight of our offensive linemen.” And Stafford said: “We’re down to the third center.”

The Rams still won 34-6. The offensive line even got an A-plus from Blaine Grisak of Rams On SI. But now the bill has come due.

Coleman Shelton: out four to six weeks with a hamstring strain.

Dylan McMahon: placed on injured reserve with a knee injury, which some reports describe as a partial patella tear. That means he’s out at least four games.

Steve Avila: still dealing with his own hamstring injury.

Part two: why the center is so important

Casual fans see the center as the guy who snaps the ball and then blocks. But that’s like saying a head coach is the guy who wears the headset.

The center is the quarterback of the offensive line.

Before the snap, he reads the defense. He identifies the front. He figures out who the middle linebacker is, which tells everyone how the protection should slide. Then he makes the calls, so all five linemen move together like one unit. If he gets it wrong, somebody goes unblocked, and Stafford pays the price.

After the snap, his job changes. Now he’s facing defensive tackles who weigh over 300 pounds and want to push him straight into the quarterback’s lap. He has to hold his ground against bull rushes. He has to handle twists and stunts, where defenders cross paths to confuse the blockers.

In the run game, he’s part of the double-team blocks that move defenders off the ball and open running lanes for Kyren Williams and Blake Corum.

So when a team loses its center, it’s not just losing one blocker. It’s losing the guy who organizes the whole line.

Part three: why a hamstring is tough for a lineman

Now let’s talk about the injury itself.

A hamstring might not sound like a big deal for a lineman. They’re not sprinting downfield like receivers. But it matters more than you’d think.

Every snap is an explosion out of the stance. Every play involves pushing off, sliding sideways, and keeping your base under you while a 300-pound defender tries to knock you over. The hamstring is the engine behind a lot of that movement.

Here’s the careful part. The four-to-six-week number is a reported estimate. It’s not a guaranteed return date. Hamstrings can heal faster than expected. They can also linger.

So let’s do the math. The Rams play the Packers on Wednesday, November 25. Then the Chiefs. Then the 49ers on December 13. If Shelton is out four to six weeks, there’s a real chance he misses all three of those games.

This is not a report, it’s my estimate based on the reported timeline: I’d plan for Shelton to miss the Packers, Chiefs, and 49ers, and anything earlier would be a bonus.

Part four: why the McMahon injury makes it worse

Here’s where this goes from “unfortunate” to “uh-oh.”

Dylan McMahon did exactly what a backup is supposed to do. He came in, he played, and McVay said he “did a nice job.” And then he got hurt too.

Think about how this normally works in the NFL. Your starter goes down, and you say, “No problem. Next man up.” But that only works if the next man is ready, available, and knows the job.

When your starter and your backup are both out, you’re not just substituting a player. You’re reorganizing the whole unit.

And on an offensive line, everything is connected. Move one guy, and you change the assignments next to him. Change the assignments, and you change the communication. Change the communication, and the rhythm of the whole offense can wobble.

That’s the domino effect. The first domino was Avila. The second was Shelton. The third was McMahon. And the Rams have to make sure no more fall.

I’m not saying the Rams have no options. They do. But the margin for error just got a lot smaller.

The IR math gets tighter

There’s another layer to the McMahon move that a lot of people will miss.

NFL teams only get eight return designations per season. That’s how many times a team can bring a player back from injured reserve. And the Rams were already running low.

Think about who’s on IR now and could come back this year: Puka Nacua, Davante Adams, Terrance Ferguson, Kobie Turner, Jordan Whittington, and now Dylan McMahon. That’s a lot of players who could all need one of those designations.

So every new IR move makes the math harder. If the Rams use up all their designations, a player who gets healthy late in the season might not be able to come back, even for the playoffs.

That’s why Shelton not going on IR matters too. A four-to-six-week injury could have been an IR move. But the Rams seem to be keeping him on the active roster for now, which saves a designation. That’s my read on it, not something the team has explained.

Part five: Beaux Limmer is the name to watch

The name to circle in red is Beaux Limmer.

Limmer has experience at center. And that’s the most important thing right now. The worst thing you can do in this situation is ask someone to learn the position in one week. Calls, footwork, snap timing, reading the defense. Those are skills you build over time.

And we already saw Limmer do it. In Arizona, he started the game at left guard and then slid to center in the middle of a road game. PFF graded him at 75.6 for the day. McVay said: “Can’t say enough about Beaux Limmer. He starts the game at guard, slides into center.”

So on paper, Limmer is the natural choice.

But I’m not going to tell you he’s a perfect fix. Here are the questions I want answered:

Can he handle powerful defensive tackles for a full game? Playing center for a half is not the same as holding the pocket for 60 minutes against a prepared opponent.

Can he make protection calls quickly and correctly? The calls only help if they’re right and fast.

Can he stay in sync with Stafford? A quarterback and center share a language. Mistakes in that language show up as missed blocks, false starts, or a bad snap at the worst moment.

Can he keep the run game working? Kyren and Corum need the middle of the line to open holes.

And here’s the biggest question. If Limmer moves to center full-time, who plays left guard? Fix one problem, and you might create another.

Part six: the left guard question

This is where it gets tricky.

If Limmer is at center, somebody has to play left guard. And Avila, the usual starter at that spot, is still dealing with his hamstring.

In Arizona, Bill Murray came in at left guard. PFF graded him at 67.0. That’s okay, not great. And Stafford praised the backups for being able to “not get a lot of reps in practice and go in there and execute in the game.”

Then there’s David Quessenberry. He’s an experienced veteran lineman. In Arizona, McVay used him as an extra tight end for some snaps. In a stretch like this, experience is gold. Coaches love a guy who’s been around, understands what a game plan needs, and doesn’t panic when things get messy.

The question is how McVay sees him. Is Quessenberry a guard option? A tackle backup? Or a sixth lineman in big formations?

Let me be clear: that’s analysis, not a confirmed plan. I don’t have inside information on what McVay is going to do, and I won’t pretend I do.

This is not a report, it’s my guess: I think Limmer starts at center against the Packers, Murray starts at left guard unless Avila is ready, and Quessenberry is the extra lineman in heavy packages. We’ll find out when McVay talks this week.

Part seven: chemistry versus the best five

McVay has a real choice to make here.

Option one: protect the chemistry. Offensive lines are all about continuity. Five guys who play together build a feel for each other that you can’t fake. So maybe you keep as many people in their usual spots as possible.

Option two: find the best five. Maybe you shuffle everyone around and ask, “Which five guys give us the strongest line?” Even if it means more moving parts.

Both have risks.

If you protect the chemistry, you might start someone at center who isn’t your best option.

If you reshuffle everything, you might lose the comfort the line has built.

And remember, the Rams only have a short bye week to figure this out. McVay called it “an accelerated bye,” because the Packers game is on Wednesday. So there’s not much practice time to build new chemistry.

We’ve seen this movie before

Rams fans with long memories are probably getting flashbacks right now.

Back in 2022, the year after the Super Bowl, the Rams’ offensive line was wrecked by injuries. They used a long list of different line combinations. Shelton himself suffered a high ankle sprain that year. Stafford took a beating and missed games late in the season. And the Rams finished 5-12.

So yes, offensive line injuries can sink a season. We’ve seen it happen to this exact team.

But here’s why 2026 feels different, at least so far.

The run game is working. In 2022, the Rams couldn’t run the ball. This year, they have their best four-game rushing edge since 1984.

The backups have already proven something. In Arizona, the line got an A-plus with backups all over the field.

The defense is rising. In 2022, the defense couldn’t carry the team every week. This year, it held Arizona to 123 total yards.

McVay is adapting faster. The two-back look, the three-tight-end sets, the quick screens. McVay is already building an offense around what he has.

None of that guarantees anything. But it’s a reason to believe this won’t be 2022 all over again.

Part eight: what this means for Stafford

Now let’s talk about the most important person in this story: Matthew Stafford.

Let me say this clearly so there’s no confusion. Stafford is not injured. Nobody is saying his season is in danger.

The danger is exposure. More pressure. More hits. A harder job.

And here’s why the center matters so much for a quarterback. Not all pressure is the same.

Edge pressure comes from the outside. A quarterback can often step up in the pocket or slide away from it.

Pressure up the middle is different. It comes straight at the quarterback, and there’s nowhere to step.

When interior pressure shows up, a lot of bad things happen:

It can ruin his throwing platform, so he can’t plant his feet and drive the ball.

It can force him to throw before the routes develop.

It can collapse the pocket from the inside.

It can mess up play-action, which depends on the line selling the run and then holding up long enough for the fake to work.

It makes it harder to step into deep throws.

And it cuts down the time receivers have to get open.

Think about how many of Stafford’s big plays in Arizona came from play-action. The 44-yard catch by Mumpfield. The catch by Parkinson down to the 1. The Davis Allen touchdown. All of those needed the line to hold up.

Stafford is one of the best passers of his generation. But even a great quarterback is only as good as the time he’s given.

Part nine: Stafford doesn’t need perfect, he needs dependable

Here’s my main point.

Stafford doesn’t need perfect protection. No quarterback gets that. But he needs dependable protection. He needs to trust that the pocket will be there when his back foot hits the ground.

And the center is also his first line of communication. When defenses show disguised blitzes and complicated fronts, the center has to help figure out the picture and make sure everyone knows what to do.

The good news? Stafford is one of the best in the league at handling this himself. He’s 38, he’s in his 18th season, and he’s seen just about every blitz there is. McVay said before the Arizona game that Stafford is “truly an extension of the coaching staff” and that he can “give him information, and he’s able to echo and articulate it to the other 10.”

So Stafford can help a new center. He can make some of the calls himself. He can get the ball out faster. That’s a huge advantage that a young quarterback wouldn’t have.

But if defenses can get pressure up the middle, the Rams lose part of the biggest advantage of having Stafford: the ability to pick a defense apart when he’s comfortable.

Part ten: what it means for the run game

This isn’t only about the passing game. Let’s talk about Kyren and Corum.

The center is a key piece of the run game.

On inside zone runs, he’s working combination blocks with the guards.

On gap runs, he helps create the seams.

On double teams, he’s the anchor that moves a defensive tackle off the line.

If a new center is learning the job, the chemistry with the guards takes time. The timing with the running backs takes time.

And remember how good this run game has been. The Rams have outrushed opponents by 478 yards over the last four games. That’s their biggest four-game rushing differential since 1984, when Eric Dickerson ran for 2,105 yards. In Arizona, Corum, Kyren, and Rivers combined for 44 carries and over 200 rushing yards.

Defensive coordinators watch film. If they see a weakness in the middle, they’ll attack it. And if the Rams can’t run up the middle, the offense can get predictable. Predictable offenses get stopped.

Part eleven: the flip side

But I want to be fair, because there’s another way to look at this.

This could also be an opportunity.

The Rams just proved in Arizona that they can win with backups on the line. They scored 31 straight points after Shelton and McMahon went down. The line got an A-plus. And the run game closed out the game.

McVay said after the game: “We’re never victims. We’re not going to go that route.” He talked about “confidence earned through evidence.” Well, Limmer and Murray just gave him some evidence.

And if McVay leans into the run game even more, Kyren and Corum could become more important than ever. A strong run game takes pressure off the quarterback, shortens the game, and lets the offensive line be the aggressor instead of the one on its heels.

So there are two paths. One is a line that loses its rhythm and an offense that sputters. The other is a team that adapts, leans on its running backs, and wins with grit.

Based on the last four weeks, I know which path I’m betting on.

Part twelve: how McVay could help the line

So what can McVay do to help a patched-together line? Here are a few ideas. This is my analysis, not McVay’s plan.

Quick throws. Get the ball out of Stafford’s hands fast. Screens, slants, and short passes to the backs. Remember, the Xavier Smith screen was one of the biggest plays in Arizona.

More two-back and three-tight-end looks. Extra blockers help. The Rams used 20 personnel with Kyren and Corum together, and 13 personnel with three tight ends. Both can give the line extra help.

Move the pocket. Bootlegs and rollouts can get Stafford away from interior pressure.

Lean on the run game. The best way to slow down a pass rush is to make it worry about the run.

Use Quessenberry as an extra blocker. McVay already did that in Arizona.

McVay said the bye is time to “creatively come up with new ideas based on who’s available and who’s not.” Expect a lot of those ideas to be about protecting the middle of the line.

And they’ll have film of the Rams’ patched-together line from Arizona. If they see a weakness in the middle, they’ll go after it.

So this game might come down to one question: can the Rams’ new interior hold up?

Part fourteen: the bigger picture

Let’s zoom out.

The Rams are 6-4 with four straight wins. They’re winning with a great run game, a rising defense, and depth players stepping up.

But the injury list is long. Puka Nacua is on IR. Davante Adams is on IR, with McVay saying “after Green Bay is a realistic timeline.” Terrance Ferguson is on IR. Now Coleman Shelton is out four to six weeks, Dylan McMahon is on IR, and Steve Avila is still hurt.

That’s a lot. But this team has handled a lot already. Since losing to Arizona 18-16 in October, the Rams have won four straight. McVay said the adversity has “calloused us the right way.”

And on the other side of the ball, the defense is getting healthier, with Trent McDuffie and Kam Curl back in the secondary.

So yes, this is a real problem. But it’s not the end of the world. It’s another test. And this team has passed every test for the last month.
