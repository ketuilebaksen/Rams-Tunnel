Part two: McVay reinvented the offense in one week

This is the stat that blew me away.

Before the Arizona game, the Rams had used 11 personnel on 53% of their plays this season. That’s one running back, one tight end, and three wide receivers. It’s the most common formation in the NFL, and it’s been the Rams’ bread and butter for years.

Against Arizona? They ran only three plays from 11 personnel. Three.

Think about that. More than half of their plays all season came from one formation, and in this game, they basically threw it away.

Why? Because Puka Nacua was out with knee and toe injuries. Davante Adams was out with a hamstring injury. And tight end Terrance Ferguson was out with hamstring and ankle issues. McVay didn’t have the receivers to run his normal offense. So instead of forcing it, he built a new one.

Why dropping 11 personnel is such a big deal

Let me explain why this matters, in case personnel numbers are new to you.

The first number is how many running backs are on the field. The second number is how many tight ends. Whatever’s left, out of five eligible receivers, is wide receivers.

So 11 personnel means one back, one tight end, three receivers. 13 means one back, three tight ends, one receiver. 20 means two backs, zero tight ends, three receivers.

Most NFL teams use 11 personnel more than anything else. The Rams under McVay have been one of the most 11-heavy teams in football for years. It’s the base of everything they do. The play-action, the crossing routes, the screens. It’s all built off that look.

So when McVay goes from 53% of his plays in one formation to just three plays in a game, that’s not a small tweak. That’s like a chef throwing out his signature recipe the night of a big dinner and cooking something brand new. And then everyone loves it.

That’s the kind of thing only a coach who really knows his own system can pull off. You have to understand why every play works to know how to rebuild it with different players.

13 personnel. One running back and three tight ends. McVay said before the game that it “was going to be a big part of the game plan.”

20 personnel. Two running backs, zero tight ends, and three receivers. This was the new one. The Rams hadn’t used it all season.

And here’s the number. According to NFL Next Gen Stats, the Rams ran 17 plays out of 20 personnel for 197 yards and a touchdown.

McVay explained the idea after the game. He said 20 personnel was “a way to be able to get two of our best players on the field at the same time, with Blake and Kyren.” And he said it was “something different that we hadn’t put on tape yet.”

And it worked. Arizona’s defense had no film on it. They had no way to prepare.

How the franchise tag works

Since Graziano mentioned the franchise tag, let’s explain what that is, because a lot of fans hear it and assume it means a player is leaving.

When a player’s contract runs out, he becomes a free agent and can sign anywhere. But teams get one tool to stop that: the franchise tag. If a team tags a player, he gets a one-year contract at a very high salary, based on what the top players at his position make. And the team keeps his rights for another year.

There are two main types. With the exclusive tag, no other team can negotiate with him. With the non-exclusive tag, other teams can make him an offer, but his original team can match it. If they don’t match, they get two first-round picks as compensation.

So “tag him and listen to offers” doesn’t mean the Rams are trying to get rid of Puka. Teams often tag players simply to buy more time to work out a long-term deal. It’s a business tool, not a goodbye.

And a lot of the time, the tag never even gets used, because the team and the player agree on an extension first.

Part eleven: why I think this hypothetical is very unlikely

Now let me give you my opinion. This is not a report. It’s my take.

I think the chances of the Rams letting Puka walk, or tagging him and shopping him, are very low. Here’s why.

One: he’s only 25. He’s young. Most of his best years should still be ahead of him.

Two: his chemistry with Stafford. Stafford trusts Puka like he trusted Cooper Kupp. That kind of connection is hard to build.

Three: his production. 70 catches and 715 yards in seven games. That’s a pace most receivers can only dream of.

Four: the Rams’ history. Les Snead and McVay have generally paid their stars when they believe in them. Aaron Donald. Cooper Kupp. Matthew Stafford. They don’t usually let elite players walk away in their prime.

Five: the replacements. The Rams just traded away their 2027 first-round pick. Finding another Puka is not easy.

Now, could the injury concerns affect his next contract? Maybe. Teams think about durability when they decide how much guaranteed money to give a player. That’s fair. But there’s a big difference between “his contract might be structured carefully” and “the Rams are going to let him go.”

As a Rams fan, I want Puka healthy and here for years. And I think the Rams do too.

I think Puka can learn that balance as he gets older. A lot of great players do. And that’s a conversation for the offseason, not a reason to panic right now.

If I were Les Snead

Let me play general manager for a minute. This is not a report. It’s just how I’d think about it.

If I’m Les Snead, I’m not thinking about letting Puka go. I’m thinking about how to keep him in a way that protects the team.

That could mean a contract with a lot of guaranteed money up front, but with some protection later in the deal, in case injuries keep piling up. A lot of teams do that with players who have injury history. It’s fair to both sides.

It could also mean having a conversation with Puka about playing a little smarter. Not softer. Smarter. Pick your battles. Don’t take a big hit on a play that doesn’t matter.

And it means building an offense that doesn’t fall apart without him. That’s actually what McVay just proved in Arizona. The Rams can win without Puka for a few weeks. That’s good for the team, and it also means Puka doesn’t have to rush back before he’s ready.

That’s how a smart team handles a star with an injury history. You protect him and you protect yourself. You don’t let him walk.
