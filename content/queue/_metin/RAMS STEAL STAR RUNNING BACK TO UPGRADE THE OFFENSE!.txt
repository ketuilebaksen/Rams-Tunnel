Let’s start with the numbers, because Kyren Williams’ season looks very different when you lay it out week by week.

Week 1 in Melbourne against the 49ers: 11 carries for 41 yards and a touchdown, plus three catches for 24 yards. It was a rough night for the whole team — the Rams lost 27-7 — and Kyren scored their only touchdown.

Week 2 against the Giants: 12 carries for 85 yards. That’s 7.1 yards per carry. He added two catches for 12 yards and a receiving touchdown.

Week 3 in Denver: 15 carries for 88 yards, and six catches for 70 yards. That’s 158 yards from scrimmage in a game where Puka Nacua and tight end Terrance Ferguson were both out.

Week 4 in Philadelphia: 16 carries for 80 yards and two rushing touchdowns — including the game-winner with 1:10 left — plus a career-high ten catches for 67 yards. He led the Rams in targets and receptions that day. Not Davante Adams. Not Puka. Kyren.

Week 5 against Buffalo: 16 carries for 49 yards and the game-tying touchdown with five seconds left in regulation, plus six catches for 27 yards.

Add it all up, and through five games Kyren has 70 carries for 343 yards — 4.9 yards per carry — with four rushing touchdowns. And through the air, he has 27 catches for 200 yards and another touchdown. That’s five total touchdowns in five games. And notice the trend in his carries: 11, then 12, then 15, then 16, then 16. His role has grown every single week.

And remember where Kyren came from, because it makes this evolution even more impressive. The Rams drafted him in the fifth round in 2022 out of Notre Dame — 164th overall. He wasn’t supposed to be a star. He was undersized, he wasn’t a burner, and in his very first NFL game, a Thursday night opener against the Buffalo Bills, he suffered a high-ankle sprain. Think about that. His NFL career started with an injury against the same team he just tied the game against on Monday night with five seconds left. He fought his way back, he won the starting job in 2023, and he put together back-to-back 1,000-yard rushing seasons in 2023 and 2024. Every year, people have said the Rams need to replace him. Every year, he’s found a new way to make himself more valuable.

Now let me tell you why those receiving numbers matter so much. Twenty-seven catches in five games puts Kyren on pace for more than 90 catches over a full 17-game season. For a running back, that’s a massive number. And it changes how defenses have to play the Rams.

Think about what defenses were doing earlier this season. When Puka Nacua was out, they rolled coverage toward Davante Adams. They dared Matthew Stafford to beat them with anyone else. And Stafford, at times, forced the ball into tight windows — that’s part of why he had six interceptions through the first four games. But a running back who can catch six, eight, ten passes a game gives the quarterback a safety valve. A check-down that gains seven yards instead of a forced throw into double coverage. That’s not a small thing. That’s the difference between a drive that keeps going and a drive that ends with a turnover.

And look at what happened against Buffalo. Stafford threw 54 passes and didn’t throw a single interception — his first interception-free game of the season. Kyren caught six of those passes. Corum caught three more, including a 34-yard catch-and-run. When the running backs are reliable receivers, the quarterback doesn’t have to be a hero on every snap.

Pro Football Focus noticed this, too. According to PFF, through four weeks, Kyren had an 85.7 rushing grade and an 81.4 overall grade — the best overall grade among all Rams offensive players at that point. Think about that. On an offense with Stafford, Puka and Davante, the highest-graded offensive player was the running back.

And if you’ve followed McVay’s career, this should feel familiar. Back in 2017, his first season as head coach, Todd Gurley became one of the most dangerous dual-threat backs in football. He caught 64 passes for 788 yards that year and won Offensive Player of the Year. McVay knows exactly how much a running back who catches the ball can stretch a defense. He built his first great Rams offense around that idea. And now, nearly a decade later, he’s leaning on it again with Kyren. I’m not saying Kyren is 2017 Todd Gurley. But the blueprint is the same: make the running back a weapon in the passing game, and the entire offense becomes harder to defend.

And Kyren’s catches aren’t just check-downs, either. Look at the final drive of regulation on Monday night. Down seven with 1:39 left, Stafford hit Kyren over the middle to help set up the goal-line situation, and then Kyren punched in the tying touchdown himself. In Philadelphia, he caught ten passes and scored the game-winning touchdown with 1:10 left. When the Rams have needed a play in the last two minutes of their last two games, the ball has gone to Kyren.

Now, here’s the honest part, because there’s one weakness in Kyren’s game right now. According to that same PFF data, his pass-blocking grade through four weeks was just 32.5 on 37 pass-block snaps — the lowest among Rams players with at least 30 pass-blocking snaps. That matters, because the more he’s on the field on passing downs, the more he has to pick up blitzers. If Kyren can tighten up his pass protection, he becomes a true three-down weapon. If he can’t, defenses are going to keep sending pressure right at him.

So why is everyone talking about a “star running back” coming to Los Angeles? Let me explain why a running back trade almost certainly isn’t happening — and why the Rams probably don’t need one.

Reason number one: the money. The Rams went all-in this offseason. They traded for Myles Garrett. They traded for Trent McDuffie. And according to the latest reporting, they have roughly one million dollars in salary cap space. One million. You can’t trade for a star running back with that. Even a mid-level veteran would require the Rams to send salary back or restructure a contract — and every contract they could restructure belongs to a player they need.

Reason number two: the draft picks. The Garrett trade cost the Rams their 2027 first-round pick, their 2028 second-round pick and their 2029 third-round pick. The McDuffie trade cost them the 29th pick in the 2026 draft, plus a fifth and a sixth that year and a 2027 third-rounder. That’s a lot of future capital already gone. Would the Rams’ front office spend more draft picks on a running back when the backfield is already producing? I seriously doubt it.

Reason number three: the Rams actually moved a running back out this summer. On August 27th, they agreed to trade running back Jarquez Hunter to the Miami Dolphins for wide receiver Tutu Atwell. Hunter was a 2025 fourth-round pick out of Auburn. When McVay made that deal, he praised his depth backs, Jordan Waters and Dean Connors, saying they had “solidified” themselves as real factors in the backfield. That tells you how the Rams view their running back room. They felt comfortable enough to trade one away.

And reason number four: Kyren Williams is already locked in. He signed a three-year, 33 million dollar extension with the Rams. They’ve already made their investment at the position. They’re not shopping for a new lead back.

So when would a running back move actually make sense? Honestly, the only realistic scenario is an injury. If Kyren or Corum went down for an extended period, the Rams would lean on Jordan Waters and Dean Connors first — the two backs McVay praised this summer. Only if that depth wasn’t holding up would they look outside the building, and even then, it would be a minimum-salary veteran or a practice squad signing, not a star. That’s the reality of a team with about one million dollars in cap space. Every dollar is committed, and it’s committed to players like Garrett, McDuffie, Stafford, Puka and Davante — the stars who give this team its Super Bowl ceiling.

Now, let me be crystal clear about something. This is not a report. If the Rams did look for running back help before the November 10th trade deadline, I’d expect it to be a cheap, veteran, pass-protecting back — someone to handle third downs and short-yardage blitz pickup. Not a star. Not a blockbuster. But right now, there’s no credible reporting connecting the Rams to any running back. And I’m not going to invent a name just to get clicks.

Now let’s talk about the other half of this backfield, because Blake Corum’s story this season is fascinating.

Corum came into 2026 with real momentum. The Rams drafted him in the third round, 83rd overall, out of Michigan in 2024 — the same Blake Corum whose 17-yard touchdown run in overtime of the Rose Bowl against Alabama sent Michigan to the national championship. Last season, he played in all 17 games and finished with 746 rushing yards and six touchdowns. He spent the offseason training to be more explosive, and his workout videos drew plenty of attention in July.

And Week 1 backed it up. Ten carries for 54 yards. That’s 5.4 yards per carry. According to the Rams’ official website, only six running backs with at least ten carries had a better average that week. Four of his ten runs went for eight yards or more. After the game, Corum said: “I definitely feel more explosive.” And he added: “I consider myself an explosive back.” McVay said: “I thought Blake did a nice job with the opportunities that he did have.” And offensive coordinator Nate Scheelhaase said Corum “picked up where he left off last year as far as being able to hit those runs.”

But then his workload started to shrink. Ten carries in Week 1. Twelve in Week 2. Six in Week 3. Five in Week 4. Five in Week 5. In Philadelphia, his snap share dropped to just 15.5 percent — down from 45.2 percent in Week 2 against the Giants. Before the Bills game, The Athletic’s Nate Atkins wrote that the Rams need more from Blake Corum.

And then on Monday night, Corum made his case. Five carries for 19 yards and a one-yard touchdown that tied the game at 13 early in the third quarter. And three catches for 53 yards — a season high in receiving yards — including a 34-yard catch-and-run that was the longest reception of his NFL career. That’s eight touches and 72 total yards. That’s efficiency.

And here’s another thing to remember about Corum: his value. He signed a four-year rookie contract worth about 5.76 million dollars in total when the Rams drafted him in 2024. For a team that’s right up against the salary cap, that’s incredibly valuable. A back who ran for 746 yards last season, who averaged 5.4 yards per carry in Week 1 this year, and who costs a fraction of what a veteran would — that’s exactly the kind of player a cap-strapped contender needs. If anyone on this roster is a “steal,” it might be Blake Corum.

So here’s my question for McVay. If Corum gives you 72 yards on eight touches, why only eight touches?

There’s a balance to strike here, and I understand both sides. Kyren is the lead back, he’s the better pass catcher, and he’s the guy McVay trusts with the game on the line. You saw it in Philadelphia and you saw it against Buffalo — when the Rams needed a touchdown in the final minutes, they handed it to Kyren. But a 16-carry, six-catch workload every single week is a lot for a back who isn’t the biggest guy on the field. The Rams want Kyren fresh in December and January. Getting Corum more involved — even eight to ten carries a game — keeps Kyren sharper for the moments that matter most.

Now let’s be honest about the run game against Buffalo, because it wasn’t great.

Before the game, a lot of previews — including some I saw from other channels — said the Rams should pound the ball against Buffalo. The Bills had allowed over 100 rushing yards to every opponent they’d faced. And the idea was that Kyren could get 20 to 25 carries and wear down the Bills’ front seven.

That didn’t happen. Kyren had 16 carries for 49 yards — 3.1 yards per carry, his lowest of the season. Corum added 19 yards on five carries. And the Rams ended up throwing 54 times. Now, part of that was the game script. Buffalo kept scoring, the Rams were chasing points, and Puka was beating every coverage the Bills threw at him. When your receiver is catching 17 passes, you keep throwing. But part of it was also that the Rams just couldn’t consistently move the Bills’ front on the ground.

Meanwhile, on the other side, Buffalo ran for 234 yards. James Cook had 111 and two touchdowns. Josh Allen ran for 121. That’s the contrast that decided the game as much as any field goal. The Bills could run. The Rams couldn’t, at least not consistently.

But here’s the bigger picture, because one bad rushing night doesn’t define a season. According to EPA data from Weeks 2 through 5, compiled by WalterFootball’s matchup hub, the Rams ranked fifth in the NFL in rushing offense. Fifth. Earlier in the year, Kyren was averaging 7.1, 5.9 and 5.0 yards per carry in Weeks 2, 3 and 4. The run game isn’t broken. It had one tough night against a defense that knew the Rams were going to throw.

And here’s one more important detail: Kyren hasn’t had 20 carries in a single game this season. His high is 16. That’s not because he’s struggling. It’s because the Rams are using him differently. Instead of 22 carries and two catches, he’s getting 16 carries and six to ten catches. The total number of touches is still high. The touches are just spread between the backfield and the passing game. That’s the upgrade. That’s what makes Kyren a different weapon in 2026 than he was in his first few seasons as the Rams’ lead back.

Let me put that in one number. Seventy carries plus 27 catches equals 97 touches in five games. That’s almost 20 touches a game. Kyren is still the engine of this offense — he’s just getting there in a smarter way. Fewer hits between the tackles, more touches in space where he can make defenders miss. For a running back who’s taken a lot of punishment over the last few seasons, that might be the best thing McVay could do for his long-term health. A smarter workload in October is how you keep a running back fresh in January.

Now let’s talk about what’s changing around the backfield, because the blocking situation matters for both backs.

First, the tight ends are getting healthier. Colby Parkinson returned against Buffalo after missing the Eagles game with knee and shoulder injuries. Tyler Higbee has been productive. Davis Allen made one of the biggest catches of the night in overtime. Terrance Ferguson is on injured reserve with an ankle sprain, but the Rams still have enough bodies to run their heavy formations. And when the Rams line up with multiple tight ends, the run game opens up.

Second, there’s a change on the offensive line this week. Left tackle Alaric Jackson was suspended for his postgame altercation with Bills linebacker Bradley Chubb. The league originally gave him two games, but on Wednesday, according to the Rams’ official website, the suspension was reduced to one game on appeal. So David Quessenberry will start at left tackle on Sunday. That affects the run game, too — Jackson is a key part of the Rams’ left-side run blocking. Watch whether the Rams lean more on runs to the right side this week, or whether they trust Quessenberry to hold up on the left.

And third, there’s some good news on the depth front. McVay said offensive lineman Keagen Trost, who’s been on injured reserve with a hamstring injury since the preseason, is expected back this week. More healthy linemen means more flexibility, and that helps both running backs.

So let’s look at Sunday, because this is a perfect chance for the Rams’ run game to bounce back.

The Arizona Cardinals come to SoFi Stadium at 1:05 Pacific time. Arizona is 1-4. The Rams are 12.5-point favorites, according to FanDuel’s line as reported by Sports Illustrated. And look at the matchup data from WalterFootball’s matchup hub. The Cardinals’ defense ranks 26th in the league in EPA per play. Their pass defense ranks dead last — 32nd — in dropback EPA. The Rams’ offense ranks fifth in EPA per play. On paper, this is one of the most lopsided matchups on the Week 6 schedule.

Here’s why that matters for the running backs. When a defense is that vulnerable through the air, it has to commit extra defenders to coverage. That leaves lighter boxes. And lighter boxes are exactly what Kyren and Corum need to get back on track. If the Rams get an early lead, they can lean on the run in the second half, protect Stafford with Jackson out, and give Corum the extra carries he’s been missing.

And don’t forget what Puka Nacua’s return does for the running game. When Puka was out, defenses could put an extra safety over Davante Adams and still have enough bodies to stop the run. Now they have to account for a receiver who just caught 17 passes in one game. You can’t double Puka, double Davante and still load the box against Kyren. Something has to give. On Monday night, Buffalo kept the run game in check, and Puka made them pay for it all night long. If Arizona chooses to bracket Puka, the running lanes should open up. Either way, the Rams’ offense has a counter for whatever a defense decides to take away. That’s what makes this group so dangerous when it’s healthy.

There’s also a defensive storyline in this game that affects the backfield. The Rams’ run defense just gave up 234 yards to Buffalo. The best way to protect a defense that’s been struggling against the run is to keep it off the field. Long, physical drives — the kind Kyren and Corum can produce together — shorten the game, keep Arizona’s offense on the sideline and give the defense a chance to rest. A balanced offense doesn’t just help Stafford. It helps Chris Shula’s defense, too.

So here’s what I want to see on Sunday.

Number one: balance. Not 54 pass attempts. Somewhere around 25 to 30 runs between Kyren and Corum. Against a 1-4 team with a struggling defense, the Rams should be able to control the clock and close the game out on the ground.

Number two: Corum with at least eight carries. He’s been efficient when he gets the ball. Give him a real role. Let him be the closer in the fourth quarter. It keeps Kyren fresher and gives defenses a different look.

Number three: Kyren in the passing game — but with clean pass protection. If he’s going to be on the field on third downs, he has to win his blitz pickups. With Jackson out at left tackle, the backs’ protection becomes even more important.

Number four: finish in the red zone. On Monday night, every Rams touchdown came from five yards or closer, and two of those five came from the running backs. Keep that going.

Now let me give you my grades for the running back room through five games.

Kyren Williams: A-minus. Five total touchdowns, 543 yards from scrimmage, and a real evolution as a receiver. The only things holding him back from an A are the pass protection and the tough night against Buffalo.

Blake Corum: B. Efficient when used, explosive when given space, and coming off his best receiving game as a pro. But his workload has been too inconsistent to give him a higher grade — and some of that is on the coaching staff, not on him.

The running back usage overall: B-minus. The Rams have found a great formula with Kyren as a dual threat. But they need to find more snaps for Corum, and they need to stay committed to the run when the game allows it.

And the idea of trading for a star running back: incomplete — because it isn’t happening, and it doesn’t need to. The Rams already have the upgrade in their own building.
