Part one: the pregame fear, laid out

Let's be fair to the pregame worry first, because it wasn't crazy.

Here's what the Rams were missing. Puka Nacua, done for the year after MCL surgery and a three-game suspension. He had 70 catches in seven games, the most ever through seven games. Terrance Ferguson, done for the year after ankle surgery. Davante Adams, still on injured reserve with his hamstring. Steve Avila and Coleman Shelton, both out on the offensive line. And Jordan Whittington was inactive too.

So the receivers were Tutu Atwell, Konata Mumpfield, Xavier Smith, and CJ Daniels, with Tyler Higbee as the top target.

On the other side, the Packers came in 6 and 4, off a bye, with a defense that had 14 interceptions, second most in the league. Safety Xavier McKinney had six of them alone. Green Bay was plus five in turnover margin. The Rams were minus three.

And then there was Parsons. A pass rusher who had at least 12 sacks in each of his first five seasons, coming back from a torn ACL, against a Rams line with Justin Dedich making his season debut at left guard and Beau Limmer making just his second start at center since the 2024 playoffs.

On paper, that's a scary list. And the original video was right to be worried. It even said, fairly, that projections aren't prophecies.

So what happened?

Part two: how the game actually went

The first half was a fight. The Rams opened with a field goal. The Packers punched back. Parsons got his first sack of the season late in the first half. Stafford threw an interception to linebacker Ty'Ron Hopper, which gave Green Bay the ball in Rams territory. But the defense held, and Green Bay didn't score off it.

That's a big moment, and it's easy to miss. Against a team that lives on turnovers, giving the ball away in your own territory is usually exactly how you lose. The Rams' defense erased it.

Then the Rams got a takeaway of their own. Tight end Tucker Kraft fumbled, and the Rams turned it into three points.

13 to 13 at the break.

And then the second half started, and the Rams put together one of the best drives of the McVay era. Their first possession of the half lasted more than eight minutes, and it ended with a Blake Corum touchdown.

Eight minutes. Think about what that does. The Packers' offense sits on the bench and gets cold. Their defense is on the field play after play, getting hit by a 32-carry back. And the clock disappears.

After that, the Rams' defense took over. Jaylen McCullough picked off a deep Love pass. Emmanuel Forbes picked off Love again late, with Green Bay out of timeouts. And Green Bay finished the half with two first downs.

Rams 26, Packers 13.

Part three: Blake Corum, 32 for 184

Let's talk about the star of the night.

Blake Corum, 32 carries, 184 yards, a touchdown. Career highs. And 151 of those yards came in the second half alone.

Let's put that in context. Before this game, Corum was averaging 5.3 yards per carry with six touchdowns. Last night he averaged about 5.75 yards per carry on 32 attempts. Usually, the more carries a back gets, the lower his average goes, because the defense knows what's coming. Corum went the other way. He got better as the game went on.

And there's a streak here too. Coming into the Packers game, Corum had scored in six straight games. With the touchdown last night, that's seven straight.

Here's what I love about this. A year ago, people were asking if Corum was just a short-yardage guy. Last night, he carried a playoff-caliber offense through an entire half against a top-ranked defense. That's not a short-yardage guy. That's a lead back.

And he didn't do it alone. Kyren Williams split the work with him on two long drives that together covered more than 11 minutes. That's the two-back plan we talked about after Arizona. The "pony" idea. Two backs, both healthy, both fresh, wearing a defense down.

Part four: why the run game was the answer

Remember what we said in the last video. Without Puka, the Rams wouldn't have one star replacing him. They'd have a system.

Last night, that system was the run game.

And here's the chain. Rams run the ball, and the clock moves. The clock moves, and the Packers' offense waits. The Packers' offense waits, and Jordan Love gets fewer chances. Fewer chances, and the pressure on Love goes up. More pressure, and you get forced throws. Forced throws, and you get interceptions.

That's exactly how the second half played out. Two first downs for Green Bay. Two interceptions by Love.

And look at Stafford's second half. He threw only eight passes after halftime. Eight. That's not a sign the passing game failed. It's a sign McVay didn't need it. When the run is working like that, you don't take risks against a defense with 14 interceptions. You keep handing it off.

That's smart football. You don't play into the other team's strength. Green Bay's defense lives on turnovers. So the Rams took away the chances for turnovers by keeping the ball on the ground.

Part five: the Green Bay run defense myth

Here's the part that surprises me.

Before the game, the original video said the Packers had been solid against the run, allowing about four yards per carry, and that their defense ranked sixth in DVOA entering Week 10.

So Green Bay was supposed to be able to handle the run. And the Rams still put up a 184-yard back.

Why? A couple of reasons. First, the Rams' run game has been historic. Over the four games before this one, they outrushed opponents 653 to 175. ESPN Research said that was the franchise's best four-game stretch since 1984, the Eric Dickerson year. Second, heavy personnel. When the Rams go with two backs or extra tight ends, they get more blockers at the point of attack. And third, the Rams just kept coming. A defense can handle 15 carries. Thirty-two carries is a different thing.

So this wasn't a fluke. It was the identity this team has built over the last month.

Part six: the offensive line answered the question

Now let's talk about the group that worried everyone the most. The offensive line.

No Avila. No Shelton. Dedich making his season debut at left guard after missing the first 10 weeks with a hand injury. Limmer at center. And Micah Parsons on the other side.

And the line paved the way for 32 carries and 184 yards from one back, plus Kyren on top of that.

Yes, Parsons got a sack. He's Micah Parsons. He's going to get one. But one sack and one bad first-half moment isn't a broken line. A broken line gives up five sacks and stops the run cold. Last night's line controlled the second half.

That's a huge credit to Dedich, to Limmer, and to whoever is coaching that room. When you lose two starters and still dominate the line of scrimmage for a half, that tells you the depth is real.

And there was one more worry the line had to carry. The Rams also had a few early exits during the game, including tight ends Davis Allen and Tyler Higbee, according to the recap. So the blocking in the second half came with even fewer bodies. That makes it more impressive.

Part seven: the defense did exactly what we said it would

Let's go back to what we said before the game.

The pregame read was simple. The Rams' defense had allowed just three touchdowns over the previous five games. They're top five in defensive EPA per play and EPA per pass, according to Sports Illustrated. And they had the full pass rush together: Myles Garrett, Aaron Donald, Kobie Turner, and Byron Young, plus Braden Fiske. In the white throwbacks, honoring the old pass-rushing quartet.

And here's how it played out against Jordan Love: 17 of 29, 198 yards, one touchdown, and two interceptions.

Let's decode that. 17 of 29 is under 60 percent. 198 yards on 29 throws is under seven yards per attempt. And two interceptions against one touchdown. For a quarterback who usually gets the ball out in about two and a half seconds, that's a defense that disrupted his rhythm.

Remember what the original video said. Love gets the ball out faster than anyone, which takes away the pass rush. So how do you beat that? You cover tight. You take away the first read. And that's exactly where the press-man trend with Trent McDuffie and the secondary pays off. When the first read isn't there, even a quick-throw quarterback has to hold the ball. And when he holds it against Garrett and Donald, bad things happen.

Two interceptions. One from McCullough on a deep ball. One from Forbes to finish it.

The Micah Parsons debut

Let's give Micah Parsons his due, because his return is a real story.

Parsons tore his ACL last season. Coming back from that injury is a long road, and last night was his first game of 2026. And he got a sack late in the first half. That's a reminder of who he is. A player with at least 12 sacks in each of his first five seasons doesn't need much time to make an impact.

But here's the bigger picture. One sack, in a game where the Rams ran the ball again and again, is a sign the Rams handled him. The best way to slow a great pass rusher is to make him play the run. Every handoff to Corum is a play where Parsons can't pin his ears back and rush. He has to set the edge, read the play, and chase. That takes away his biggest weapon.

That's exactly what McVay did. He didn't try to out-pass Parsons. He made Parsons tackle running backs for a half.

And for Green Bay fans, I'll say this with respect. A healthy Parsons makes the Packers dangerous in January. The Rams just happened to have the perfect plan for him on this night.

Stafford's night, honestly

Let's be fair to Matthew Stafford too, because his line won't jump off the page.

He threw the interception to Hopper in the first half. And he only threw eight passes in the second half. I don't have his full stat line from the recap, so I won't make one up.

But here's what matters. Coming into the game, Stafford was completing 66.7 percent of his passes for 2,623 yards, 19 touchdowns, and 10 interceptions. He's been the steady hand all season. Last night, his job was different. It was to manage the game, avoid the big mistake in the second half, and let the run game work. He did that.

A veteran quarterback knows when the game doesn't need him to be a hero. Last night was one of those nights. And on Thursday against Kansas City, I expect he'll be asked to do more.

Part eight: the turnover flip

This is my favorite stat of the night.

Before the game, the Packers were plus five in turnover margin. The Rams were minus three. That was supposed to be one of Green Bay's biggest advantages.

Last night, the Rams forced three turnovers: Kraft's fumble and Love's two interceptions. They gave up one, Stafford's pick to Hopper.

So the Rams won the turnover battle three to one. Against the team that came in as one of the best in the league at winning that exact battle.

And here's why it matters for the rest of the season. Turnover margin is one of the strongest predictors of winning close games in December. If this defense can keep forcing turnovers, the Rams don't need a perfect offense. They need a solid one. And they have that.

Part eleven: the counterargument

Let me give you the skeptic's case, because I don't want this to be pure celebration.

The skeptic says: the Rams' passing game didn't prove anything. Stafford threw an interception, and he only threw eight passes in the second half. The Rams won because they ran the ball, but you can't run 32 times every week. Kansas City and San Francisco will stack the box. And when they do, who's going to beat them through the air? Atwell? Mumpfield? Smith? And now Higbee and Allen got hurt too. The tight end room was already thin without Ferguson.

That's a fair argument. And honestly, it's the right question for the Chiefs game.

Here's my answer. First, the Rams don't need to pass 40 times. They need to pass well when they do. After Arizona, McVay pointed out that nine different players caught passes on 18 completions, and he said this offense is at its best when "a bunch of different people" are involved. Second, Adams is coming. A healthy Adams changes how defenses play the Rams, even at 33. Third, the run game makes the pass easier. When defenses stack the box to stop Corum and Kyren, that opens play action. And Stafford with play action and one-on-one coverage is still one of the most dangerous passers in football.

So yes, the passing game is the question. But it's a question this team has answers to.
