The Rams and the 49ers just traded players — without making a trade. In the span of one week, Los Angeles waived its punt returner, Xavier Smith, and San Francisco scooped him up and put him on its practice squad. And at almost the same time, the Rams went the other way and signed Eric Kendricks — a linebacker who started both of San Francisco’s playoff games last January. Two NFC West rivals, two players crossing the border in opposite directions. But here’s the part nobody is talking about. One of these players is tied to the most painful moment of the Rams’ last season. Because the last time Xavier Smith stood under a punt in a game that really mattered, it was the NFC Championship in Seattle — and the ball hit the turf. So today we’re not just going through two transactions. We’re talking about why the Rams let Smith go, what that tells you about how seriously this team is taking its special teams problem, what San Francisco sees in him, and why the Kendricks move looks even smarter in that light. Let’s start with Xavier Smith, because his story with the Rams is longer and more emotional than a lot of fans realize. Smith was an undrafted free agent out of Florida A&M — a walk-on in college who turned himself into one of the best receivers in the SWAC. In his final college season, he led the conference with 87 catches for over a thousand yards and 11 touchdowns. He signed with the Rams in 2023, spent that year on the practice squad, got waived, came back, got waived again, came back again. The definition of grinding for a roster spot.

And then came his NFL debut, in Week 3 of 2024 — against, of all teams, the San Francisco 49ers. Tie game, under a minute left, and Smith returned a punt 38 yards to midfield to set up the Rams’ game-winning field goal. In his very first game. Against the 49ers. That’s the kind of moment that earns a young player a real shot, and he got one. Over his Rams career, Smith played in 34 regular-season games, caught 20 passes for 309 yards, and appeared in five postseason games. He became the Rams’ return man.

And then came January 25th, 2026. The NFC Championship Game at Lumen Field in Seattle. A trip to the Super Bowl on the line. In the third quarter, Smith signaled for a fair catch on a Michael Dickson punt, lost his balance, and the ball came loose. Seattle recovered at the Rams’ 17-yard line, and on the very next play, Sam Darnold threw a touchdown to Jake Bobo to make it 24-13. Making it worse, Smith had already muffed an earlier punt in that same game — that one he recovered — and McVay left him in. McVay later called the fumble “costly.” The Rams fought back with touchdowns from Davante Adams and Puka Nacua, but Stafford’s fourth-down pass fell incomplete with under five minutes left, and Seattle won 31-27. Seattle went on to win the Super Bowl.

And remember how close that 2025 team really was. They went to Carolina in the wild card round and won 34-31. They went to Chicago in the divisional round and won 20-17 in overtime. Two road playoff wins, both decided by a field goal. That’s a team that knew how to survive in January. And then, one game from the Super Bowl, the margins finally caught up with them. That’s the thing about the third phase of the game: you can survive mistakes for weeks, but eventually one of them shows up at the worst possible moment.

I know that’s a painful memory, Rams House. But you can’t understand this week’s moves without it. Because that game wasn’t just one bad play. It was the final chapter of a special teams nightmare that hung over the whole 2025 season. In Week 16, a 58-yard punt return touchdown against the Rams cost them the NFC West title and the number one seed — which is exactly why the NFC Championship was in Seattle instead of at SoFi. The front office fired special teams coordinator Chase Blackburn. They replaced kicker Joshua Karty with Harrison Mevis. And the problems still didn’t go away.

So fast forward to this season. Smith opened 2026 as the Rams’ returner and played the first three games. And on October 2nd — two days before the Eagles game — the Rams waived him. The new special teams coordinator, Bubba Ventrone, didn’t sugarcoat it. He called Smith “inconsistent” and said the team needed better ball security. Sean McVay put it even more simply: “We just need better production out of that position.” McVay said the decision was made late the night before, and he talked to Smith face to face the next morning. That’s how you handle a player who has given you everything for three years — honestly, directly, and in person.

And the Rams didn’t stop there. To replace Smith, they promoted veteran receiver Alex Bachman from the practice squad to handle punt returns. And McVay made it clear that while he trusts Kyren Williams anywhere on the field, he didn’t want his starting running back back there unless he absolutely had to. That tells you how thin the options were — and how much the Rams wanted a sure-handed veteran over a big-play gamble.

Bachman is a 30-year-old receiver who has bounced around the league — he started his career with the Rams, then spent time with the Texans, the Raiders and the Giants before coming back to Los Angeles. He’s not a burner, and he’s not going to be on many highlight reels. His career return numbers are modest. But that’s not why he got the job. He got it because a veteran who’s seen every kind of punt — the knuckleballs, the high hangers, the ones that drift toward the sideline — is more likely to make the safe decision. Fair catch when you need to. Let it bounce when you should. Secure the ball every time. That’s the job description right now.

Here’s my honest take on the Smith decision: it was the right call, and it was a hard one. Smith has real speed and real talent, and he’s a genuinely likable story — a walk-on turned NFL player. But a returner’s first job isn’t to make highlights. It’s to secure the football. When your special teams coordinator uses the words “inconsistent” and “ball security” in the same answer, and when your team’s last season ended partly because of a muffed punt, you can’t keep waiting. Special teams lost the Rams games last year. They cost them points in Denver and Philadelphia this year. Something had to change.

And I want to take a second to explain why the return job is so brutal, because it’s easy to yell at a returner from your couch. A punt returner stands alone, looking straight up at a spinning ball, with eleven players sprinting directly at him. He has to judge the flight, the wind, the spin, decide in a split second whether to catch, fair catch or let it go — and then, if he catches it, he gets hit immediately. One mistake, and it can swing a game. One mistake in the wrong game, and it can swing a season. That’s why coaches value calm, sure-handed returners so highly, even over faster ones. A returner who never gains a yard but never loses the ball is often worth more than a returner who breaks one big play a month and fumbles twice.

Now — San Francisco. On October 7th, the 49ers announced they’d signed Smith to their practice squad. And it makes sense from their side. The 49ers have been dealing with injuries at receiver, and they’ve been looking for speed and return experience. Smith gives them both. He’s around 5-foot-9, about 180 pounds, and he ran in the 4.4 range in his pre-draft testing. He knows how to return kicks and punts at the NFL level. He’s played in big games. For a practice squad signing, that’s a reasonable bet.

But I wouldn’t expect much more than that. He’s not going to become a big part of San Francisco’s offense, which runs through their established playmakers. If he plays at all, it’ll be on special teams. And the ball security question doesn’t disappear just because he changed jerseys. The 49ers know exactly what happened in Seattle last January. They’re betting on the speed and the experience, and accepting the risk.

And the timing on San Francisco’s end is interesting too. The 49ers signed Smith right before their showdown in Seattle on Sunday — the 4-0 49ers against the 3-1 Seahawks, in the same stadium where Smith’s muff happened last January. Will he be elevated for that game? We’ll see. It’s not something I’d predict. But if he is, and he ends up fielding a Michael Dickson punt at Lumen Field again, the whole Rams House will be watching for very different reasons.

And there’s a little irony here worth pointing out. Xavier Smith’s NFL career began with a game-winning punt return setup against the 49ers. Now he’s a 49er. And the Rams and 49ers meet again on December 13th at Levi’s Stadium. Could he be standing back there to field a Rams punt in that game? It’s possible. Football has a funny way of writing those storylines.

Now let’s flip it around, because while San Francisco was picking up a Rams player, the Rams were picking up a 49ers player — and I think the Rams got the better end of this unofficial swap.

We covered the Kendricks signing in detail in our last video, so I’ll keep this tight. Eric Kendricks is a former first-team All-Pro and Pro Bowl linebacker from his years in Minnesota. He’s 34 now, in his 12th NFL season. He has roughly 1,180 career tackles and 21 and a half sacks. He played for the Chargers in 2023 and the Cowboys in 2024, where he had 138 tackles. And last November, when Fred Warner went down for the season, the 49ers signed him to their practice squad. He worked his way onto the active roster and then started both of San Francisco’s playoff games.

And here’s the detail that connects everything. In the wild card round last January, the 49ers went into Philadelphia and beat the Eagles 23-19 — and Kendricks started at linebacker, with a big day in the tackle column. One week later, the 49ers went to Seattle and got crushed 41-6 by the same Seahawks team that went on to end the Rams’ season and win the Super Bowl. So Kendricks has seen it all: the road playoff win in Philly, the blowout loss in Seattle, the NFC West at its most brutal. That’s the kind of experience you can’t teach.

And look at the contrast between the two moves. The Rams let go of a young, fast player whose biggest problem was consistency with the football. And they added a veteran whose biggest strength is consistency — a reliable tackler, a communicator, a player who knows where to line up and what’s coming. That’s not a coincidence. That’s a philosophy.

And it’s not just Kendricks. Look at the pattern over the last few weeks. Out goes a young, inconsistent returner; in comes a veteran returner in Bachman. A veteran linebacker with playoff experience joins the practice squad. Even on offense, the Rams have leaned on their veterans to steady things — Davante Adams carrying the passing game while Puka was out, Tyler Higbee making key catches in the comeback in Philly. When a team with Super Bowl expectations starts 2-2, the instinct is to chase the big splash. This front office is doing the opposite: plugging the small leaks, one by one. After a 2025 season that ended because of mistakes in the margins, and a 2026 start full of special teams leaks and turnovers, this front office is choosing reliability over upside on the edges of the roster.

Is Kendricks going to give the Rams the 49ers’ secrets? No. Let’s be real about that. Teams change terminology and calls all the time, and a linebacker doesn’t walk out of the building with a playbook. But he does bring something real. He knows how San Francisco’s defense prepares and communicates. He knows the personnel he practiced against every day — their receivers, their tight ends, the tendencies you only notice from inside the building. When the Rams prepare for the 49ers in December, having a player who just lived in that building is a small edge. In the NFC West, small edges decide division titles. Ask the 2025 Rams, who watched one December special teams disaster cost them the division.

And think about where the Rams are right now. They’re 2-2, behind the 49ers at 4-0 and the Seahawks at 3-1. They already lost to San Francisco in Melbourne in Week 1. That December 13th rematch at Levi’s could very well decide the division. Every little advantage the Rams can build between now and then — a healthier roster, a cleaner special teams unit, a veteran linebacker who knows the opponent — matters.

And don’t forget who else lives in this division. The Seattle Seahawks aren’t just 3-1 — they’re the defending Super Bowl champions. They beat the Patriots in Super Bowl LX at Levi’s Stadium in February, a few weeks after ending the Rams’ season. So the NFC West right now has the defending champs in Seattle, an undefeated 49ers team, and a Rams team sitting on top of the Super Bowl odds board at 2-2. There is no easy path. Every roster spot, every returner, every backup linebacker is part of a fight that will probably go down to the final weeks of the season.

Now let’s talk about what all of this means for Monday night against Buffalo, because that’s the next game and it’s a big one. On special teams, the Rams will have their reshuffled return unit going up against a Bills team that has its own problems. And remember, in Philadelphia, the special teams leaks continued: a 52-yard punt return allowed to Britain Covey that set up the Eagles at the Rams’ 17-yard line, a missed 48-yard field goal by Mevis, and a kickoff that went out of bounds. Getting rid of a returner doesn’t fix coverage units or kickers. The Smith move solves one problem. It doesn’t solve all of them. Against a quarterback like Josh Allen, short fields are deadly. The Rams’ special teams have to be clean on Monday night — not great, just clean.

And on defense, Kendricks gives the Rams a safety net. He’s on the practice squad, so he’d need to be elevated to play, and nobody is promising that he will. But with Aaron Donald questionable and injuries in the linebacker room, the Rams now have a veteran option sitting right there if they need him. Against a quarterback who scrambles and extends plays like Allen does, a sure tackler in the middle of the field is never a bad thing to have in your pocket.

Here’s my bottom line, Rams House. On the surface, this looks like two small practice squad moves that most people will scroll past. But together, they tell a story. The Rams let go of a player who reminds everyone of the most painful play of last season — and they did it because ball security matters more than speed in the third phase of the game. And they added a veteran who just spent a playoff run inside the building of their biggest division rival. One move is about learning from the past. The other is about preparing for December. That’s what a contender does.

And as for Xavier Smith — I’m not going to root against him. He worked incredibly hard to get to this league, and he gave the Rams some real moments, including that debut against the 49ers. I hope he gets his career back on track. Just not on December 13th.

And let’s give him his due, because his story is one worth respecting. A walk-on at an HBCU who led his conference in receiving, went undrafted, survived multiple cuts, and ended up playing in five playoff games for the Los Angeles Rams. Most players with that résumé never get one NFL snap. Xavier Smith got three seasons, a game-winning moment against the 49ers, and a run to the NFC Championship. One bad afternoon in Seattle doesn’t erase that. It just means the Rams, with their Super Bowl window wide open, couldn’t afford to wait for him to fix it.
