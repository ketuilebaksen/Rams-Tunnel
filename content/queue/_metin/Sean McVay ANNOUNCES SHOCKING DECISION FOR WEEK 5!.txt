About one million dollars. And the Rams don’t have their 2027 first-round pick. They don’t have their 2027 third-round pick. They don’t have their 2027 seventh-round pick. And they don’t have a single compensatory pick projected for next year. This is the general manager who built a Super Bowl champion with “F them picks.” The man who traded for Matthew Stafford, Jalen Ramsey, Von Miller, Trent McDuffie and Myles Garrett. But this time, the bank account might really be empty. Let’s start with the money, because that’s the first wall Snead runs into. According to Kenneth Arthur of Turf Show Times, using Over the Cap’s numbers, the Rams have roughly a million dollars in available cap space for 2026. That’s barely anything in NFL terms. A single veteran’s prorated salary can eat that up. So if the Rams want to add any player with a meaningful contract before November 10th, they’d have to create room first.

And here’s a detail most fans don’t know about midseason trades. Player salaries are paid out week by week over the regular season. So when a team trades for a player at the deadline, it only has to fit the remaining portion of that player’s salary under the cap, not the whole thing. That’s why deadline deals can look “cheaper” than offseason deals. A player making, say, ten million dollars might only cost around half of that against the cap after the deadline. But even half of a big contract is far more than a million dollars. For the Rams, any acquisition with a real salary means creating room first. There’s no way around it.

How do teams create room in the middle of a season? The most common tool is a restructure. You take part of a player’s base salary, convert it into a signing bonus, and spread that bonus out over future years of the contract. The player gets the same money — often faster — and the team’s cap hit drops this year. The catch? That money doesn’t disappear. It gets pushed into future seasons, where it becomes dead money if the player is released or traded later. It’s a credit card. Useful in the moment, expensive down the road.

Arthur identified five Rams with significant base salaries who could theoretically be restructured: Davante Adams at 18 million, A.J. Jackson at 18.9 million, Matthew Stafford at 16 million, Kevin Dotson at 8 million, and Kyren Williams at 5.5 million. Four of those five could be restructured. Dotson is the exception — he’s set to become a free agent in 2027, so the only way to lower his cap number would be a contract extension.

And here’s where it gets tricky. Look at those names. Stafford is 38 years old. Davante Adams is a veteran in the later stages of his career. Pushing more of their money into future years means betting that they’ll still be on the roster — and still be worth it — down the line. Arthur himself said he wouldn’t add more future money to those deals just to make a deadline move. I tend to agree. If the Rams restructure, it has to be for a player who changes their Super Bowl odds — not for a depth piece.

And there’s a bigger picture here, too. Garrett is under contract through 2030. McDuffie was expected to sign a long-term deal after the trade. Puka, Kyren, Byron Young and the rest of the young core will all want to get paid at some point. Every dollar the Rams push into the future today competes with those future contracts. The Rams aren’t just managing this season’s cap — they’re managing the next four or five years of a roster built around expensive stars. Snead has to win now without mortgaging the moment Stafford walks away.

Now — the draft picks, because this is the second wall, and it might be even bigger. Let’s walk through where the Rams’ future picks went.

First, the Myles Garrett trade in June. The Rams sent Cleveland edge rusher Jared Verse, their 2027 first-round pick, their 2028 second-round pick and a 2029 third-round pick. And that 2029 third-rounder comes with a fascinating condition: according to CBS Sports, it becomes a first-round pick if the Rams ever trade Garrett to an AFC North team — the Steelers, Ravens or Bengals — before the 2029 draft. The Browns basically built a wall to make sure Garrett never shows up in their division wearing someone else’s jersey.

And let’s not forget what Jared Verse represented. He was a first-round pick in 2024 and won Defensive Rookie of the Year — a young, cheap, ascending pass rusher who could have been a cornerstone for a decade. The Rams gave him up, plus three premium picks, because they believed Garrett gives them a better chance to win right now. That’s the definition of going all-in. And it’s exactly why the Rams can’t afford to make a deadline trade that doesn’t move the needle. When you’ve already pushed this many chips into the middle of the table, every additional chip has to count.

Second, the Trent McDuffie trade in March. To get McDuffie from Kansas City, the Rams sent the 29th overall pick in the 2026 draft, plus 2026 fifth- and sixth-round picks, and a 2027 third-round pick.

Third, the Tre’Davious White trade, which cost the Rams their 2027 seventh-round pick.

Add it all up, and according to Over the Cap, the Rams are missing their own first, third and seventh-round picks in 2027, with no compensatory picks projected. They still have some ammunition — their 2027 second-rounder, for example — but when you’ve already traded away your first-round pick, every other pick becomes more precious. And opposing general managers know that. When you’re negotiating from a short stack, the other side asks for more.

Now, let me be clear: none of this means those trades were mistakes. Garrett was coming off an NFL-record 23 sacks in 2025, and he’s under contract through 2030. McDuffie is a 25-year-old cornerback who was one of the best in football in Kansas City. These are exactly the kinds of moves a contender should make. When FOX Sports graded the McDuffie trade, they called it “F them picks 2.0” and gave the Rams an A-minus. Snead summed up his whole philosophy with one question: “What edge does that player give this version of the Rams?” That’s the right question. The problem is that after asking it so many times, the Rams have fewer chips left to play.

So who’s out there? Let’s go through the names being floated — and I want to be clear before I start. This is not a report. None of these players has been linked to the Rams by any team source. These are simply players that analysts have listed as possible trade candidates around the league.

Name number one: Marvin Harrison Jr., the Arizona Cardinals receiver. PFF included him on its list of 15 possible trade candidates for the 2026 deadline. On paper, he’s a fascinating name — a former top-five pick with elite size and route-running pedigree. But Arthur was blunt about him: he argued that Harrison simply hasn’t been very good, and that his reputation from the draft is bigger than his production. And there’s another obstacle: Arizona is a division rival. Trading a former top pick to the Rams, a team you play twice a year, is the kind of deal front offices hate making. I’d file this one under “fun to talk about, very unlikely to happen.”

Name number two: Brian Thomas Jr. of the Jaguars. Also on PFF’s list. Thomas had a big rookie season, but according to Arthur, he’s been average since then, if not disappointing — just 105 receiving yards this season at the time the article was written. And there’s a contract question: a player in that position will eventually want a big extension, and the Rams don’t have the cap room for that kind of commitment. Arthur compared it to the Jerry Jeudy trade to Cleveland — a team paying a premium price for a receiver whose draft reputation outran his production.

Name number three: Daron Payne, the defensive tackle. PFF listed him as a possible trade candidate too. Payne made the Pro Bowl in 2022, but Arthur described his career since then as relatively quiet, and questioned why he’d even be available if he were still playing at that level. For the Rams, I’m not sure the defensive line is the biggest need right now — not with Aaron Donald trending toward a return, Kobie Turner and Braden Fiske playing well, and Garrett on the way back.

So what are the Rams’ real needs? Let’s be honest about it. First, receiver depth behind Puka Nacua and Davante Adams. Puka has been dealing with a groin issue — one outside doctor has even raised the possibility of a sports hernia repair down the road — and the drop-off behind the top two is steep. Second, tight end. Terrance Ferguson is on injured reserve with a severe ankle sprain, and Colby Parkinson has been banged up. Third, linebacker depth, where injuries have piled up this week. And fourth, special teams, which have cost this team points in Denver and Philadelphia.

And here’s what’s interesting: the Rams have already been addressing those needs — just not with blockbuster trades. They promoted veteran Alex Bachman to fix the punt return job. They signed former All-Pro linebacker Eric Kendricks to the practice squad. Odell Beckham Jr. — a familiar name — is sitting in free agency after the Giants released him. Free agents don’t cost draft picks. Practice squad veterans don’t cost draft picks. With a million dollars of cap space and no first-rounder, that’s the lane the Rams are probably going to live in: smart, cheap, low-risk additions.

Even those cheap additions require planning, though. A veteran signed in the middle of the season only counts his remaining prorated salary, which helps — but with roughly a million dollars of room, the Rams can’t sign more than a player or two without making some kind of adjustment. Every roster spot, every practice squad elevation, every minimum contract has to be part of a plan. That’s what life looks like for a team that went all-in during the spring.

But here’s the part that I think most people are missing. The Rams’ biggest deadline addition might not be a trade at all. It’s Myles Garrett.

Garrett had arthroscopic knee surgery after the Week 1 loss in Melbourne and went on injured reserve. According to reporting from NFL insider Ian Rapoport, he was expected to miss about six weeks — which points to a return around Week 9, against Washington on November 8th. Two days before the trade deadline. There’s even been talk that he wants to come back sooner, possibly as early as Week 7 against the Raiders, though the Rams are being cautious. Think about that. Right around the deadline, the Rams are going to add a pass rusher who just set the NFL single-season sack record. No team in the league is going to make a deadline trade for a player like that. The Rams don’t have to. He’s already theirs.

And when you combine Garrett’s return with Aaron Donald getting healthy, Jaylen Watson coming back from his shoulder, Puka back on the field, and Parkinson trending up, the Rams are about to have the healthiest, most complete roster they’ve had all season — right around the deadline. That’s the version of the team the front office built in the spring.

And think about the timeline from Stafford’s point of view. He’s 38. The McDuffie trade was made with the understanding that this is a win-now window, and FOX Sports framed it exactly that way — a push for a Super Bowl while the quarterback is still playing at an MVP level. Every move the Rams made this year was about maximizing the next few months. So the question isn’t whether the Rams want to win now. They clearly do. The question is whether one more trade actually increases the chances more than simply getting this expensive, talented roster healthy at the same time. In a way, the deadline “move” is simply getting everyone on the field together for the first time.

So what would I do if I were Les Snead? Here’s my plan. Step one: no big restructures for depth players. Save the restructure option for one thing only — a true difference-maker if one suddenly becomes available at a fair price. Step two: keep working the margins. A veteran receiver on a minimum deal, a tight end who can block and catch, special teams help. Step three: let the medical staff, not the calendar, decide when Garrett and Puka are ready. A healthy Garrett in January is worth more than any player you could trade for in November. And step four: protect the 2028 and 2029 picks that are left. This window is open now, but Snead has to make sure it doesn’t slam shut the moment Stafford retires.

And let me say this clearly: if the Rams don’t make a big trade before November 10th, that’s not a failure. That’s discipline. The worst deadline deals are the desperate ones — the teams that trade future firsts for a player who doesn’t fix their actual problem. The Rams already made their big bets in the spring. Now they need those bets to pay off.

Of course, Snead has surprised us before. Remember 2021, when the Rams traded for Von Miller and signed Odell Beckham Jr. in the middle of the season — and won the Super Bowl? That’s why nobody should ever say never with this front office. If a star becomes available, Snead will at least pick up the phone. He always does. But this time, the cupboard is thinner than it’s ever been, and the math is unforgiving.

And don’t forget the competition. The Rams aren’t the only contender looking for help before November 10th. The 49ers are 4-0 and leading the NFC West. The Seahawks are the defending Super Bowl champions. Teams with more cap space and more draft picks can outbid the Rams for any player who becomes available. If a star receiver suddenly hits the trade market, the Rams might not even be able to make the most competitive offer — not because Snead doesn’t want to, but because he’s already spent the currency. That’s the trade-off of being aggressive early: you get your stars first, but you have less left when the late opportunities show up.

And remember, before any of this matters, there’s a football game on Monday night. The Rams host the Buffalo Bills at SoFi in a matchup between the top two teams on the Super Bowl odds board. A win puts the Rams over .500 and makes the deadline conversation about fine-tuning a contender. A loss would make the pressure to “do something” much louder. That’s how quickly the narrative changes in this league.

Here’s my bottom line, Rams House. With roughly a million dollars in cap space, no 2027 first-round pick and no compensatory picks coming, I would not expect a blockbuster before November 10th. Marvin Harrison Jr., Brian Thomas Jr. and Daron Payne are interesting names, but each one comes with real questions — about production, about contracts, about whether they’d even fix the Rams’ biggest problems. The smartest move this front office can make is the one it’s already making: get healthy, add smart veterans on the margins, and let Myles Garrett be the deadline acquisition nobody else can match.
