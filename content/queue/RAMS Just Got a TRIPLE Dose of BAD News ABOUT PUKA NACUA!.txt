Part two: why Puka is worth fighting for

Let's remind everybody who we're talking about.

Puka Nacua is 25 years old. He was a fifth-round pick in 2023, 177th overall. And he's already one of the best receivers in football.

As a rookie, he was a second-team All-Pro. He set rookie records for catches and receiving yards. Last season, he led the entire NFL in receptions. And this season, before the injury, he had 70 catches for 715 yards and four touchdowns in just seven games. That's the most catches any player has ever had through seven games.

Think about that value. A fifth-round pick is supposed to be a backup or a special teams player. Puka became a star. That's the best kind of draft pick a team can make, and it's a huge reason the Rams have been able to stay competitive while spending big on other spots.

And his connection with Matthew Stafford is a real thing. Stafford's newest contract includes $50 million in guaranteed money for 2027. The Rams are built to win right now. And keeping Stafford's best target is a big part of that.

The Rams' 2023 draft class was incredible. And that's actually the problem.

Along with Puka, the Rams drafted guard Steve Avila in the second round, and edge rusher Byron Young and defensive tackle Kobie Turner in the third round. All four have become important starters. And all four need new contracts for 2027.

Steve Avila anchors part of the offensive line. And we just saw how much the Rams miss him. Without Avila and Coleman Shelton, the line had to patch things together against Green Bay.

Byron Young brings pressure off the edge, alongside Myles Garrett.

Kobie Turner is a valuable presence up front on the defensive line, next to Aaron Donald.

Puka Nacua is the top receiver.

So the Rams hit on four starters in one draft. That almost never happens. But the reward for hitting is that all four get paid at the same time. Rookie contracts are cheap. Second contracts are not.

This is what McVay meant when he said there are "guys that are part of his draft class" and others on offense and defense who are up. It's not just Puka. It's a whole wave.

Part four: the franchise tag explained

Now, one option the Rams have is the franchise tag. Let me explain how it works.

When a player's contract ends, his team can use the franchise tag to keep him for one more year. The player gets a one-year salary based on what the top players at his position make. And according to the report from The Athletic, the tag for Puka would cost about $31 million next year.

That's actually below the top of the wide receiver market. So on a one-year basis, it's a pretty good deal for the Rams.

But here's the catch. A team can only use one franchise tag per year. So if the Rams tag Puka, they can't tag Avila, Young, or Turner.

And there's a second catch. The tag is one year of money, all at once, against the cap. It doesn't let the Rams spread the cost out the way a long-term contract can. A multi-year extension, with a signing bonus spread over several seasons, can actually lower the cap hit in the first year. The tag can't do that.

So the tag buys time, but it doesn't give the Rams much flexibility.

Part five: extension versus tag

Let me lay out the two paths side by side.

Path one: a long-term extension. The Rams and Puka agree on a multi-year deal before March. The Rams can spread out the signing bonus, which lowers the early cap hits. Puka gets long-term security. And the franchise tag stays free for someone else, like Avila, Young, or Turner.

Path two: the franchise tag. The Rams tag Puka at about $31 million for 2027. It's one year, no long-term risk. But it uses up the only tag, it takes up a big chunk of 2027 cap space, and it can create tension, because most players want long-term security, not a one-year deal.

There's also a middle path. Teams sometimes tag a player and keep negotiating, then sign a long-term deal before the summer deadline. That happens a lot.

I'll tell you which path I'd take in a minute. But first, let's talk about the part of the report that got people talking.

Part six: the protections question

According to the report from Jonathan Jones of The Athletic, multiple sources suggested the Rams should try to include protections in Puka's next contract. Protections related to injuries, and to issues away from the field.

Let me be clear about what that means and what it doesn't.

That's a recommendation from sources, not a report that the Rams have demanded anything. There's no confirmation that the team has asked for those terms, or that anything has been agreed.

So what would "protections" look like? In NFL contracts, a few common tools exist.

Guaranteed money that becomes guaranteed later. Instead of guaranteeing everything at signing, some money becomes guaranteed only if the player is on the roster on a certain date.

Per-game roster bonuses. The player gets paid for each game he's active. If he misses games, he doesn't get that money.

Language about conduct. Contracts can include language where guarantees can be voided in certain situations, like a suspension.

Why would anyone suggest these for Puka? Because, according to the report, he's missed significant time due to injuries in two of his first four NFL seasons, and this year also brought a suspension. Those are facts teams have to weigh.

But here's the other side. Puka's agent will push back hard on all of that. A player of his caliber, at 25, with his production, is going to want big guarantees. And other teams in March would offer him guarantees without those protections. That's leverage.

So the negotiation will come down to this: how much protection the Rams can get, without pushing Puka toward free agency.

What the receiver market looks like

To understand Puka's price, you have to look at what other top receivers make.

In 2025, Ja'Marr Chase signed an extension with Cincinnati worth about $40 million a year. Justin Jefferson's deal with Minnesota averages about $35 million a year. Those are the top of the market.

So when The Athletic says a franchise tag of about $31 million is below the top of the market, that's what they mean. On a one-year basis, the tag is cheaper than what the best receivers get per year on long deals.

But a long-term deal for Puka would probably land somewhere near that top tier. He's younger than most of those players were when they signed. He's led the league in catches. And his agent will point to his record seven-game start this season.

So the realistic range for a Puka extension is big. And that's why the structure matters so much. A smart contract can carry a big average salary while keeping the early cap hits lower, using a signing bonus spread over several years.

How the Rams have handled stars before

Let's look at history, because the Rams have a clear pattern with their own stars.

They've paid big when they believed in someone. Aaron Donald got a record deal for a defensive player back in 2018. Cooper Kupp got a big extension after his triple-crown season in 2021. And Matthew Stafford has been extended more than once.

But they've also been willing to move on. When Kupp got older and more expensive, the Rams released him in 2025, and he signed with Seattle. When Jalen Ramsey's contract got big, they traded him to Miami in 2023.

So the Rams pay for stars in their prime, and they move on before the decline. And Puka, at 25, is right in the middle of his prime. That pattern points toward the Rams paying him.

The difference this time is the timing. They've never had four homegrown starters from the same draft hitting second contracts in the same year, while also paying a 38-year-old quarterback and trading away their first-round pick. That's a new kind of puzzle.

Part eight: the counterargument

The skeptic says: Puka has missed big chunks of time with injuries. He's now had a knee surgery. He's had a conduct suspension, with a warning from the league. And the Rams have four starters to pay. Part ten: why tight ends matter so much to McVay

Let me explain why this matters more for the Rams than it would for most teams.

McVay's offense uses tight ends constantly. They block in the run game. They line up in different spots to disguise plays. And they give Stafford a safe target in the middle of the field.

And think about the Packers win. The Rams ran the ball 44 times between Blake Corum and Kyren Williams. A run game like that needs blockers. Tight ends are a huge part of that.

The two-back "pony" formation helps, because it puts an extra running back on the field instead of a tight end. But you still need at least one tight end who can block on most plays.

So if Higbee and Allen can't go, McVay has a few options. He can use more two-back sets. He can use fewer multiple tight end formations. He can give Klare more snaps and trust him as a blocker, since that's what he's done so far. Or he can move targets to the receivers and running backs.

Those are possibilities, not confirmed changes. But this is the kind of problem McVay is great at solving. Remember, he just won a big game with Puka, Adams, Ferguson, Avila, and Shelton all out.

What happened to the tight end plan

Let's remember how quickly this tight end room fell apart.

At the start of the season, the Rams had Tyler Higbee, the veteran. Davis Allen, the reliable backup. Terrance Ferguson, the young second-year player with upside. Colby Parkinson, the big target. And Max Klare, the rookie.

Then Ferguson hurt his ankle against Denver in September. He worked his way back to practice in November, then pulled a hamstring during his return window. And then McVay announced his ankle needed surgery, ending his season.

And now Higbee and Allen both went down in the same game.

That's three tight ends hurt in one season. For a McVay offense that loves multiple tight end sets, that's a tough hit. But the good news is that the run game has shown it can work with two backs on the field. That gives McVay a way to adjust.

Part twelve: the schedule ahead

And the defenses are about to get tougher.

According to the report, the Rams have the second-toughest remaining strength of schedule in the NFL, behind only the Chicago Bears.

Next up is Kansas City. Then at San Francisco on December 13. Then Dallas on December 20. And Seattle twice in the final three weeks.

Here's the chain. A strong run game helps control games and takes pressure off Stafford. But good defenses will stack the box to stop Corum and Kyren. When they do, the Rams need receivers and tight ends who can punish them. And right now, the receivers are young and the tight ends are hurt.

That's why Davante Adams matters so much. Adams is getting close to returning from his hamstring injury. McVay said earlier there was "a possibility for the game after the Packers." But his exact return date is still uncertain.

If Adams comes back, Stafford gets a veteran target who can win one-on-one. And that forces defenses to back off the run.

Here's one piece of good news hidden in all of this.

Because the Rams played on Wednesday night, the night before Thanksgiving, they get extra days before their next game. That gives the medical staff more time to evaluate Higbee and Allen. It gives Adams more time to recover. And it gives McVay more time to prepare backup plans on offense.

Now, the extra time doesn't guarantee anybody gets cleared. But it's a lot better than a short week.

And it helps Corum too. Thirty-two carries is a heavy load. A few extra days of recovery matter for a running back after a game like that.

Part fourteen: what this all says about the Rams

Let me step back and connect the two stories.

Both of them are really about one thing: depth.

The Rams have won five straight while missing their best receiver, their veteran receiver, their young tight end, and two starting offensive linemen. That shows how adaptable this team is. That's coaching. That's depth.

But both stories also show the pressure coming. Right now, injuries are testing the depth. In March, contracts will test it. If the Rams pay Puka, Avila, Young, and Turner, that's a lot of money going to four players. Which means less money for depth behind them.

So the front office has a balancing act. Keep the core together, but don't spend so much on four players that the rest of the roster gets thin.

That's the real challenge of being a good team. Your players get good, and then they get expensive.
