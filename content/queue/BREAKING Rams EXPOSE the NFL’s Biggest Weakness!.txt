Sixteen times. That’s how many times the Los Angeles Rams sent an extra rusher at Jalen Hurts in Philadelphia — the most blitzes he had faced in a single game since 2024. And what happened next should scare every offensive coordinator in the National Football League. Hurts — a quarterback who, just two seasons ago, was one of the best in football against the blitz — completed only about a third of his passes when the Rams brought pressure. ESPN’s Ben Solak calculated that Hurts finished with minus 9.3 expected points added on blitzed dropbacks — the worst performance against the blitz of his entire career, by a comfortable margin. The Eagles went zero-for-twelve on third down, something that had never happened in the history of their franchise. Now, let’s define the weakness. What did the Rams actually expose?

Here’s my answer: fragile offensive lines and quarterbacks who can’t solve disguised pressure. Every season, injuries tear through offensive lines across the league. Teams start backups. Combinations that have never played together get thrown onto the field on Sunday. And most defenses respond the same way — they sit back, rush four, play zone, and hope. The Rams did the opposite. They identified a patched-together offensive line, and they attacked it with everything they had. And the result was one of the most dominant defensive performances anyone has put on film this season.

Let’s look at what Philadelphia was dealing with up front, because this matters. The Eagles were without their star right tackle, Lane Johnson. According to Athlon Sports, Markel Bell was making the first start of his career, Drew Kendall was making only his second, and it was the first time that exact offensive line combination had ever played together in a game. Center Cam Jurgens was graded by Pro Football Focus as the lowest-graded offensive lineman in the league. The Eagles’ longtime offensive line coach, Jeff Stoutland, is gone, replaced by Chris Kuper. And Hurts himself has developed a habit of holding the ball rather than throwing over the middle of the field — which means the line has to protect longer than it can handle.

Put all of that together, and you have a perfect storm. And Chris Shula saw it coming.

To understand how big this shift was, you have to go back to Week 1 in Melbourne, because this defense was a completely different unit five weeks ago. According to a film review by Rams On SI, the Rams blitzed on just 13.9 percent of snaps against the 49ers in the opener. They played zone coverage almost 78 percent of the time. That’s a defense sitting back and hoping. Brock Purdy got comfortable, the 49ers put up 379 yards, and the Rams lost. After that game, the word you heard about this defense over and over was “soft.”

And then Shula did something a lot of coordinators never do: he changed. Over the next three games — the Giants, the Broncos and the Eagles — the Rams’ blitz rate jumped to 38.6 percent, the sixth-highest in the NFL over that stretch. Their man coverage rate climbed to 36.6 percent, the fourth-highest in the league. They started dropping defensive linemen into coverage to confuse quarterbacks. Kobie Turner, a defensive tackle, has already dropped into coverage almost as many times this season as he did in all of 2025. They started overloading one side of the protection to create free rushers. In other words, the Rams went from waiting for things to happen to forcing them to happen.

And look at the results since that change. Against the Giants in Week 2, the Rams held Jameis Winston to a 37.7 passer rating and under 50 percent completions. In Denver, the defense played well enough to build a sixteen-point halftime lead — and the film review points out that Denver’s two third-quarter touchdown drives each covered 52 yards or less, because special teams mistakes kept handing the Broncos short fields. That loss wasn’t on the defense. And then came Philadelphia — the masterpiece.

Let’s walk through it. According to Rams On SI, the Rams blitzed on 53 percent of snaps against the Eagles and generated pressure on 56 percent of them. Next Gen Stats measured the blitz rate on Hurts’ dropbacks even higher — 60.6 percent — the highest of Chris Shula’s entire tenure as defensive coordinator. And on third down, the Rams blitzed on every single dropback except the very last one. Those third-down blitzes produced six pressures and a sack.

Now look at what it did to the quarterback. When the Rams blitzed, Hurts was pressured on nine of sixteen dropbacks. He completed only four of fourteen passes in those situations, according to Athlon, for one first down. And remember what I said at the top: this is a quarterback who used to destroy the blitz. In 2024, according to CBS Sports, Hurts completed over 66 percent of his passes against extra pressure for 945 yards, nine touchdowns and just one interception. Last season, against Brian Flores’ blitz-heavy Vikings defense, he went 11-of-14 for 233 yards and two touchdowns when Minnesota sent extra rushers. He was the guy you didn’t blitz. And the Rams blitzed him anyway — and broke him.

And the team numbers were historic. Zero-for-twelve on third down — a first in Philadelphia Eagles franchise history, and their most third-down attempts without a conversion since at least 1991. Hurts finished with 93 passing yards on more than 25 attempts — according to the film review, only the 14th time a quarterback has had a game like that since the merger. The Eagles scored zero points in the second half and managed only four first downs after halftime. And when they finally drove deep into Rams territory with a chance to put the game away, the defense stuffed them on fourth down at the two-yard line.

Now, here’s the part of this story I love the most. It wasn’t one player. When you watch the tape, the pressure came from everywhere — and that’s exactly what makes it so hard to solve.

Byron Young was the headliner. Over the last three weeks, he ranks seventh in the NFL in pressures, sixth in pressure rate, and fourth in sacks with three and a half. Against the Eagles, he led the team with seven pressures, and then he finished the game with two sacks on Philadelphia’s final drive — including the strip-sack on third and long that forced a fourth-and-25. Even when the Eagles tried to block him with a tight end, it didn’t work. According to the season numbers, Young alone accounts for 21 of the Rams’ 75 pressures this year.

But look at the supporting cast. Kobie Turner had five pressures from the interior. Braden Fiske was credited with more than five pressures as well. Safety Kamren Kinchens came downhill from the second level and freed up rushers — and even got a sack himself. Linebacker Omar Speights met Byron Young at the quarterback on one of the key plays. Josaiah Stewart and Kobie Turner dropped into coverage on certain snaps to disguise where the pressure was coming from. As a team, the Rams generated 26 pressures in Philadelphia.

And this is the key to the whole scheme. When a quarterback can’t tell who’s rushing and who’s dropping, he hesitates. And against a line that’s never played together before, that half-second of hesitation is the difference between a completion and a sack. The Rams weren’t just blitzing more. They were blitzing smarter — showing one look before the snap and turning it into something completely different after it.

And the coverage behind the pressure held up, which is the part that often gets forgotten. Josh Wallace, filling in for Jaylen Watson, earned the highest PFF grade on the defense in that game, allowing one catch for two yards on three targets. Emmanuel Forbes Jr. was targeted twice and allowed nothing. And across the whole season, Quentin Lake and Trent McDuffie have the two highest PFF grades on the defense — Lake at 90.0 and McDuffie at 89.1, with McDuffie allowing just five catches on 18 targets. Blitzing only works if your corners can survive on an island. The Rams’ corners have.

And the depth behind them is better than people realize. Josh Wallace, the former undrafted free agent from Michigan, can play corner, nickel and safety — the Rams call him their “Swiss-Army knife,” and defensive coordinator Chris Shula described him this week as “just unfazed.” If Quentin Lake can’t go on Monday, Wallace’s versatility gives Shula options in the back end. And in the middle of the defense, the Rams just added former All-Pro linebacker Eric Kendricks to the practice squad — a veteran who started two playoff games for the 49ers last January. A blitz-heavy defense needs bodies who understand assignments, because one wrong gap or one missed rotation turns a sack into a touchdown. The Rams have quietly built the depth to keep running this scheme even when injuries hit.

Now let’s zoom out to the whole season, because this isn’t a one-game fluke. Through four games, the Rams’ defense has allowed just 990 total yards — the second-fewest in the NFL. They allow 4.2 yards per play, second-best in the league. They’ve given up only five pass plays of 20 yards or more, tied for the fewest in football. They rank fourth in DVOA and third in EPA per play. And here’s the stat that blows my mind: since the 49ers put up 379 yards on them in Week 1, no Rams opponent has reached 260 yards. Not the Giants. Not the Broncos. Not the Eagles.

That’s why I say the Rams exposed something bigger than one bad Eagles game. The modern NFL is built around offensive efficiency, spread formations and quarterbacks getting the ball out on time. But that entire system depends on five offensive linemen working together. When injuries break up that group — and they always do, for almost every team, every season — most defenses don’t punish it. The Rams just showed what happens when a defense does. And every team with a banged-up offensive line is going to see this film.

That’s the thing about the NFL: it’s a copycat league. When one defense finds a formula that works, the other 31 coordinators study it on Monday morning. Expect to see more teams overload protections, drop defensive tackles into coverage and disguise pressure on third down after what happened in Philadelphia. But copying a plan is one thing. Having the personnel to run it is another. The Rams have fast, young edge rushers, interior linemen who can drop into coverage, safeties who can blitz, and corners who can hold up in man coverage. That combination is rare. It’s why this isn’t just a game plan — it’s an identity.

But now here’s where it gets complicated. Because Monday night, the Rams face a quarterback who might be the best in the world at beating exactly this kind of plan.

Let’s talk about Josh Allen — and the numbers are fascinating. According to the Rams On SI film review, Allen ranks ninth in the NFL against the blitz this season, at 0.11 EPA per dropback. But when teams don’t blitz him, he ranks third, at 0.28. Read that again: Allen is actually less efficient against the blitz. On the surface, that sounds like a green light for Shula to keep sending pressure.

But there’s a catch — a big one. The Rams’ blitz-heavy approach has been paired with a lot more man coverage. And against man coverage, Allen is excellent: 0.4 EPA per pass, sixth in the league. Against zone coverage, he drops to 0.12. So here’s the dilemma: blitzing makes Allen worse, but the man coverage that usually comes with the blitz makes him better. If Shula simply copies the Philadelphia plan — blitz, blitz, blitz with man coverage behind it — Allen could beat it with his arm or his legs. If Shula goes back to the soft zone of Week 1, Allen will pick it apart.

So what’s the answer? I think it’s the “zone pressure.” Send five rushers, but drop the other six into zone coverage behind them. Disguise who’s coming and who’s dropping. Make Allen hold the ball for that extra half-second while he figures it out. The film review said it plainly: the Rams have to pick their spots and balance man and zone. That’s the chess match on Monday night.

And this is exactly where Aaron Donald changes the math. The beauty of having Donald on the field is that you don’t always need to blitz to get pressure. If Donald and Kobie Turner can collapse the pocket with a four-man rush, Shula can drop seven into zone — attacking the exact situation where Allen’s numbers are weakest — and still make him uncomfortable. Then, on key downs, he can bring the blitz as a surprise instead of a habit. That’s what makes Donald so valuable even at this stage of his career. He’s played only a couple of games since coming out of retirement, and his box-score numbers have been modest. But offensive lines still have to treat him like Aaron Donald. And every blocker who turns toward number 99 is one fewer blocker for Byron Young.

Of course, Allen is not Jalen Hurts. He’s one of the most dangerous players in the sport. The last time Buffalo came to SoFi, in December of 2024, Allen threw for 342 yards and three touchdowns and ran for three more touchdowns. The Rams still won that game 44-42, with Matthew Stafford throwing for 320 yards and two scores, but it tells you exactly what Allen is capable of. A blitz that gets to him and misses is more dangerous than no blitz at all, because he turns broken plays into 40-yard gains. The Rams’ rushers have to stay disciplined in their lanes. Keep him in the pocket. Make him beat you from the pocket, not outside it.

And Buffalo’s offense is the best scoring unit in the league right now. The Bills are averaging nearly 32 points per game, first in the NFL, and they have 17 pass plays of 20 yards or more — tied for second-most in the league. That’s a direct collision with a Rams defense that has allowed only five such plays all season. Something has to give.

Now let’s talk about Buffalo’s protection, because that’s where the “weakness” angle becomes so interesting for Monday night. The Bills come in banged up across the roster. Defensive tackle Ed Oliver and receiver Joshua Palmer have been ruled out. DJ Moore is questionable with a shoulder injury, along with cornerback Dee Alford and defensive lineman Landon Jackson. The one bit of good news for Buffalo: top cornerback Christian Benford has been cleared to play after missing the Patriots game. But the bigger question is how the Bills’ offense handles a defense that disguises pressure as well as the Rams have over the last three weeks. If Buffalo’s protection holds up, Allen will find his explosive plays. If it doesn’t, we could see another version of what happened to Philadelphia.

And remember Buffalo’s last game. The Bills lost at home to the Patriots, and Allen threw the interception that sealed it — a ball his own head coach, Joe Brady, said he trusted him to throw away. Analysts have been calling Allen’s decision-making “iffy” this season, and he’s no longer the MVP favorite. A quarterback who’s pressing to make plays, against a defense that specializes in confusing quarterbacks before the snap? That’s the recipe for turnovers. And turnovers decide primetime games.

But let me be fair, because I promised you honesty. The Rams’ defense isn’t perfect, and the blitz plan has risks. First, more blitzes means more one-on-one coverage, and against a quarterback with Allen’s arm, one busted coverage can mean a 60-yard touchdown. Second, Quentin Lake’s questionable tag matters — he’s the communicator in the secondary, the guy who gets everyone lined up, and disguise doesn’t work if the back end isn’t on the same page. Third, Philadelphia’s offensive line was in a uniquely bad situation. Not every offense will be that vulnerable. And fourth, the Rams’ offense has to stop putting its defense in bad spots. The Rams have turned the ball over twice in every game this season — eight giveaways in four weeks — and Matthew Stafford has six interceptions. Special teams mistakes cost points in Denver and Philadelphia. Even the best defense in the league can’t survive short fields forever.

And that’s why this game is such a fascinating test of the entire team. The Rams’ offense has piled up 1,668 total yards — second-most in the league — and topped 400 yards in each of the last three games. But they rank just 19th in scoring. Penalties in the red zone, turnovers and special teams leaks have turned big yardage days into average point totals. If the offense cleans that up against a Bills defense that ranks 30th in EPA per play and allows almost 27 points per game, the defense won’t need to be historic. It just needs to be itself.

So let me give you my three keys for the Rams’ defense on Monday night, built around everything we just talked about. Key number one: disguise, don’t predict. Shula should keep the blitz as a weapon, but mix in zone pressures and four-man rushes so Allen never gets a clean pre-snap read. Key number two: let Donald do the dirty work. If Buffalo has to double-team Donald, the one-on-ones go to Byron Young and Kobie Turner — and that’s where sacks come from. Key number three: contain and tackle. Keep Allen in the pocket, rush in disciplined lanes, and tackle him when he runs. If the Rams do those three things, the Bills’ offense will have to grind for every yard — and against this defense, grinding hasn’t worked for anybody since Week 1.
