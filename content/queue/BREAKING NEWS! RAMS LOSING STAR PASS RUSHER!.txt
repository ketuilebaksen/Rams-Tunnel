Let's start with the gamble.

Myles Garrett is headed for knee surgery and has been placed on injured reserve. We've covered the what on this channel — now let's talk about the why, and the uncomfortable questions underneath it. Because this is not how the Rams wanted the biggest trade in franchise recent memory to begin.

First, some honesty about who the Rams are. This organization has never been afraid of the big swing under Les Snead and Sean McVay. You know the philosophy — it's practically printed on the walls of the building. When an elite player becomes available, you take the shot and worry about the downside later. We've seen it work spectacularly. The Rams don't win the Super Bowl in the 2021 season without trading for Matthew Stafford. They grabbed Von Miller midseason that same year — only had him for half a season, and he was a monster in that postseason run. Jalen Ramsey cost them a fortune in picks and became a cornerstone of the defense. Those swings built the biggest moments of the McVay era. So when Myles Garrett — the reigning Defensive Player of the Year, fresh off breaking the NFL's single-season sack record — became available, of course the Rams swung. That's who they are.

But every philosophy has a bill that eventually comes due, and right now the Rams are looking at it.

Let's walk the timeline, because when you lay it out in order, it gets uncomfortable. Earlier this offseason, the Baltimore Ravens agreed to send the 14th overall pick plus a first-round pick next year to the Raiders for Maxx Crosby. That deal died after Crosby's physical in Baltimore, when concerns emerged about his left knee. We told that full story in our earlier video — the Rams chased Crosby after that collapse, and he shut the door. So three months later, Los Angeles traded for Garrett instead.

And the warning signs showed up almost immediately. Garrett got through the final days of OTAs, but heading into the second week of training camp, the knee soreness started. From there, he was in and out of practice — dealing with swelling, held out repeatedly, never able to string together consecutive days on the field until the leadup to week one. McVay kept saying the Rams were being cautious. Then Garrett played 40 snaps in Melbourne, produced literally nothing statistically, and admitted afterward that he'd been feeling pain and didn't feel like himself. Days later: arthroscopic surgery and injured reserve.

So Rams fans are asking fair questions. Did the team know earlier that this knee wasn't responding? Could they have reached this conclusion before the season started — before putting him on a fourteen-hour flight and forty snaps against Trent Williams? And here's where I want to be careful and honest with you at the same time: nobody outside that building knows the full story. We don't know what the medical staff saw, or what was discussed between Garrett, his doctors, and the organization. What we do know is that Garrett himself said he'd been feeling pain — which makes the decision to play him in week one, in hindsight, look worse than it did at the time.

And look, there's history here, which is why some fans aren't giving the benefit of the doubt. The Todd Gurley knee saga in 2018. Stafford's elbow soreness in 2022, and the back issue the following offseason. The Rams have faced questions about injury transparency before. None of that proves anything about the Garrett situation — I want to be crystal clear about that — but it explains why part of this fanbase reads a story like this with one eyebrow raised.

There's also the flip side of the question: what did Cleveland know? The Browns traded away the Defensive Player of the Year — a guy who just put up the greatest pass-rushing season in NFL history — and got back Jared Verse plus a first, a second, and a third. Teams don't move players like that casually. Maybe it was purely about the price. Maybe. But when the player you traded away goes under the knife before week two, that question is going to hang in the air.

And notice the pattern hiding inside this whole offseason: knees. The Ravens looked at Maxx Crosby's left knee and walked away from a completed agreement — that's how seriously teams treat medical red flags on pass rushers. The Rams, months later, accepted a different pass rusher who then developed knee trouble in their own building. Two franchises, two elite edges, two knees — and only one of those teams still has its draft capital. That's not an accusation; risk assessment on trades is genuinely hard, and Garrett's knee issue may not have existed in June at all. But it shows you how thin the line is between a franchise-altering masterstroke and a cautionary tale, and how much of this business comes down to information nobody outside the building ever sees.

Now — before this turns into a funeral, let's add the context that matters. According to the reporting around the team, if the Rams could run this trade back, they would still do it. And honestly, that tracks. This isn't about declaring the trade a failure — it's way too early for that. It's about understanding the price of the gamble. Jared Verse was a promising young pass rusher and a real piece of this defense's foundation, and now he's in Cleveland — where, for what it's worth, he didn't make much of an impact in his Browns debut either. If Garrett has a clean surgery, comes back healthy, and spends November and December wrecking games, this whole conversation becomes a footnote in a story about overcoming adversity. That is exactly what Los Angeles is counting on. But until that happens, the risk is real and it is currently sitting on injured reserve.

Which brings us to the second story. Because the reason the Rams made this trade was simple: they believed Garrett could change the entire equation for this defense. Well — the equation just changed again. And that's where Aaron Donald comes in.

Think back to how Donald's unretirement felt when it happened. It felt like a bonus. A luxury. The Rams already had their front built — Garrett on the edge, Kobie Turner and Braden Fiske inside, Byron Young flying off the other side. The plan for Donald was beautiful in its simplicity: bring the legend back in a controlled, rotational role. Manage his workload. Maximize what he still has. Give the defense one more elite weapon without ever asking him to carry the unit. Donald was supposed to be the cherry on top.

Myles Garrett going on injured reserve changes that completely.

Because here's what week one exposed: without Garrett at full strength, this pass rush simply didn't have the impact everyone anticipated. Credit San Francisco — they schemed to limit the Rams up front — but the bottom line is the pressure never came. And that's exactly what Garrett was supposed to fix. When he's on the field, offenses have to account for him on every snap. Chips, slides, double teams — and all of that attention creates one-on-one opportunities for Kobie Turner and Byron Young. Take Garrett away, and those opportunities evaporate. Everyone's job gets harder at the same time.

That's why Donald's return suddenly feels enormous. Before the injury, the Rams could genuinely get by with, let's say, seventy percent of the Aaron Donald we saw before retirement — a rotational disruptor picking his spots. Now? Seventy percent might not be enough. The Rams may need Aaron Donald to be close to his best just to help this defense survive the next month. And remember the stretch we're talking about: the Giants, the Broncos, the Eagles, the Bills.

Can he do it after two years away from football? Nobody knows. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and neither is anyone in that building. Father Time doesn't lose often, and expecting Donald to immediately be the 2021 version of himself would be unrealistic. But McVay has been remarkably consistent on this: he believes Donald can play at the level he played at when he retired. And when McVay talks about Donald, he doesn't talk about nostalgia — he talks about standards, about the obsession with mastering his craft, the speed at which he learns, the work capacity, the competitive stamina. If there is one human being you'd trust to defy a two-year layoff, it's the guy who spent a decade being the most unblockable player of his generation. As we covered in our last video, the benchmarks are simple now: good practice work, and Donald saying he's ready. The expectation is that his return could come as soon as Monday Night Football against the Giants. If that happens, every camera at SoFi is going to be pointed at number 99 — not just because of who he was, but because of what this team suddenly needs him to be.

And this shift has ripple effects on the coaching staff too. Without Garrett, this defense can't just line up four rushers and expect to overwhelm people the way it was designed to. Expect more creativity — more movement up front, more blitzes, more designed pressure packages built to manufacture the one-on-ones that Garrett used to create by simply existing. That's the chess match: scheme has to replace gravity until the gravity comes back.

The good news? Los Angeles is not without weapons. Kobie Turner is a legitimate playmaker. Byron Young can genuinely get after the quarterback. Braden Fiske matters inside. The young edges get their shot. But losing Garrett while having already given up Verse means the margin for error is gone. That's the reality of the massive swing — Snead and McVay accepted it with eyes open, the same way they did with Stafford, Miller, and Ramsey. Sometimes the swing wins you a Super Bowl. Sometimes it puts you in a hole. Right now it's done both things to the same defense in the span of three months.

So here's the big picture as we head toward Monday night. The Garrett trade was always a gamble, and the Rams knew it. What they didn't expect was that chapter one of the Garrett era would involve an operating room — and that Aaron Donald's comeback would transform from a feel-good luxury into a survival necessity this quickly. But that's football. One injury completely rewrites another player's role. When Donald unretired, Rams fans were asking what he could add to a loaded defense. Now we're asking a very different question: how much does this defense actually need from him?

For now, the mission is simple. Survive. Let the surgery do its work. Let Turner, Young, and Fiske step up. Let McVay and this staff scheme pressure while the franchise waits for its record-breaker to heal. If Garrett comes back in a month and dominates when it matters most, the questions from this week become part of a comeback story. If he doesn't, this trade becomes a very different conversation. The story is far from over — but the next few weeks decide which story we're telling.
