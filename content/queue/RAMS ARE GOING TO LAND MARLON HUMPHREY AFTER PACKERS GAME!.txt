Part four: who is Marlon Humphrey?

Now let's talk about the player, because he's a real and very good one.

Marlon Humphrey was a first-round pick by Baltimore in 2017, out of Alabama. He's been a Raven his whole career. He's a physical cornerback who can play outside and in the slot, and he's famous for forcing fumbles. His "Fumble Hunter" reputation is real. He punches at the ball on almost every tackle.

He was a first-team All-Pro in 2019, and he's made multiple Pro Bowls. In 2024, he had six interceptions, one of the best seasons of his career.

So this isn't a random name. This is one of the best cornerbacks of the last decade.

But there are two things you have to consider. First, he turned 30 this past summer. Cornerbacks usually start to slow down around that age, because the position depends so much on speed and quick reactions. Second, he's had some injuries in recent years, including a foot injury in 2023. So any team looking at him in March is looking at a talented veteran with some age and health questions.

Part five: the money math, explained

Here's the key point. If his contract really ends in March, then Baltimore doesn't have to pay that number next year. And neither would anyone else. A new team would sign him to a brand new contract, at whatever price the market sets.

So the clip compares the old number to an imaginary new number that it never gives. And the original video called that out well. "Well below 26 million" isn't a number. It's a shape. Anybody who walks away with a specific price in his head made that number up himself.

Part six: how free agency would actually work

Let me explain the mechanics, because this is where a lot of fans get confused.

If a player's contract ends, he becomes an unrestricted free agent when the new league year starts in March. That means he can sign with any team. The team that signs him doesn't give up a draft pick, because there's no trade. That part of the clip is technically correct.

But there are a few catches.

Catch one: Baltimore can keep him. The Ravens could sign him to a new deal before March. Or they could use the franchise tag, although tagging a 30-year-old cornerback at a huge salary would be unusual.

Catch two: he could be released early. If the Ravens released him during the season, he'd go on waivers. After the trade deadline, every released player goes through waivers, even veterans. Teams with worse records get priority. So the Rams, at 7 and 4, wouldn't be near the front of the line.

Catch three: other teams bid too. In free agency, Humphrey picks where he wants to go. A rebuilding team might offer more money. A contender might offer a better shot at a ring.

So "the Rams pay zero picks" is true for any free agent signing. That's not special. What matters is whether the Rams want him, can afford him, and whether he wants to come. And none of that has been reported.

What a "prove-it" deal really means

The clip talks about a "short one-year deal," so let me explain why teams like those, and why players sometimes take them.

A one-year deal is often called a "prove-it" contract. The player signs for one season, usually for less money than he wants. If he plays well, he hits free agency again the next March with a fresh highlight reel and a bigger market. If he doesn't, the team walks away with no long-term money on its books.

For a team, the appeal is simple. Low risk. No dead money down the road. And a motivated veteran who's playing for his next contract.

For a player, it's a bet on himself. A player coming off a rough year, or an injury, or a controversy, sometimes takes a one-year deal to rebuild his value.

But here's the catch. The best one-year deals usually go to teams with cap space. And the Rams, as we talked about, don't have a lot of it. If they spend on a one-year corner, that's money that can't go to Puka, the offensive line, or a receiver.

So even the "cheap one-year deal" idea comes with a real trade-off.

How the Rams have built their secondary before

Let's look at history, because the Rams under McVay and general manager Les Snead have a clear pattern with cornerbacks.

They've never been afraid to go big for a star corner. In 2018, they traded for Aqib Talib and Marcus Peters. In 2019, they sent two first-round picks and a fourth-rounder to Jacksonville for Jalen Ramsey. And more recently, they traded for Trent McDuffie.

But notice something. Those were all trades for corners in their prime, or close to it. And when Ramsey got older and more expensive, the Rams traded him to Miami in 2023, for a third-round pick and tight end Hunter Long.

So the Rams' pattern is: pay for prime-age stars, and move on before the decline. Signing a 30-year-old corner coming off a rough year would be a little different from how they usually operate.

That doesn't mean it can't happen. Teams change. But if you're asking whether this fits the Rams' history, the honest answer is: not really.
