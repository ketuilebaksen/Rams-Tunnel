Let’s go back to Melbourne. The Rams lost to the 49ers in Week 1, and the defense took a lot of criticism. People called it soft. Too passive. Too much zone. And the numbers back that up. According to a film review from Rams On SI, the Rams blitzed on just 13.9 percent of snaps in Week 1 and played zone coverage almost 78 percent of the time. That’s a defense sitting back, hoping its four-man rush wins, and letting a quarterback like Brock Purdy get comfortable. And he did.

Then Shula changed. Over the next three weeks — the Giants, the Broncos and the Eagles — the Rams’ blitz rate jumped to 38.6 percent, the sixth-highest in the NFL over that span. Their man coverage rate jumped to 36.6 percent, the fourth-highest in the league. They started dropping defensive linemen into coverage to confuse quarterbacks — Kobie Turner has already dropped into coverage almost as many times this season as he did all of last season. They started overloading one side of the protection to attack weak spots. In other words, Shula stopped waiting for things to happen and started forcing them.

And it’s worth remembering who Chris Shula is. He took over as the Rams’ defensive coordinator in 2024, after Raheem Morris left to become the head coach in Atlanta. He’s young for the job, he’s been in Sean McVay’s system for years, and yes — he’s the grandson of the legendary Don Shula, the winningest head coach in NFL history. That name comes with expectations. And after Week 1, the criticism was loud: too conservative, too much zone, too easy for quarterbacks. A lot of coordinators would have dug in and defended their approach. Shula did the opposite. He looked at his personnel — a fast, young, aggressive group of pass rushers and a secondary with more man-coverage ability than people realized — and built the scheme around what they do best. That’s not just coaching. That’s humility, and it’s rarer than you’d think in this league.

And the results speak for themselves. In Week 2 against the Giants, the Rams held Jameis Winston to a 37.7 passer rating and under 50 percent completions. And then came Philadelphia — the masterpiece. According to Rams On SI, the Rams blitzed on 53 percent of snaps and created pressure on 56 percent. Next Gen Stats had the blitz rate on Hurts’ dropbacks even higher, at 60.6 percent — the highest of Shula’s tenure. And on third down, the Rams blitzed on every dropback except the very last one. Those third-down blitzes produced six pressures and a sack.

Now look at what it did to Hurts. When the Rams blitzed him, he completed barely a third of his passes. According to ESPN’s Ben Solak, Hurts finished with minus 9.3 total expected points added on blitzed dropbacks — the worst game against the blitz of his entire career, by a comfortable margin. And this is a quarterback who used to destroy the blitz. Back in 2024, he was one of the best in the league against extra pressure. On Sunday, he had no answers.

And the team result was historic. Zero-for-twelve on third down — a first in Eagles franchise history, and their most third-down attempts without a conversion since at least 1991. Hurts held to 93 passing yards on more than 25 attempts — one of only a handful of games like that since the merger. A second half with zero points and only four first downs allowed. And the goal-line stand at the two-yard line. That’s what happens when a defensive coordinator decides to stop being polite.

Now, to be fair — and we’re always fair on this channel — Philadelphia’s offensive line was a mess on Sunday. Lane Johnson was out. Markel Bell was making his first career start, Drew Kendall was making just his second, and it was the first time that exact line combination had ever played together. Blitzing a patched-together line is a lot easier than blitzing a healthy one. And Hurts has a tendency to hold the ball rather than throw over the middle, which makes him vulnerable when protection breaks down. So not every lesson from Philadelphia carries over to Buffalo automatically. But the point stands: Shula saw a weakness and attacked it relentlessly, instead of waiting to see if his four-man rush could win on its own. That’s the mindset shift.

Here’s the part I love most about this, though: it wasn’t just Byron Young. Young has been a monster — over the last three weeks, he’s seventh in the NFL in pressures, sixth in pressure rate, and fourth with three and a half sacks, plus the strip-sack that sealed the Eagles game. But the film shows a team effort. Braden Fiske was credited with more than five pressures in Philadelphia. Kamren Kinchens came downhill from the safety spot to free up rushers and even got a sack. Omar Speights met Young at the quarterback. Josaiah Stewart and Kobie Turner dropped into coverage to disguise where the pressure was coming from. When a defense is coordinated like that, quarterbacks don’t know who’s coming — and that hesitation is everything.

And through four weeks, the numbers put this defense among the best in football. Fourth in DVOA. Third in EPA per play. Second in yards allowed per game, behind only Seattle. And remember the Denver loss? The film review points out that Denver’s two third-quarter touchdown drives each covered 52 yards or less — because special teams mistakes kept handing the Broncos short fields. The defense wasn’t the problem in Denver. The field position was.

And think about what that means for the whole team. This is complementary football at its best. The defense creates short fields and three-and-outs, the offense gets extra possessions, and suddenly the pressure is off Matthew Stafford to force throws. Remember, Stafford has thrown at least one interception in every game this season, and a lot of those came when the Rams were chasing points. A defense that gets off the field on third down — like it did twelve times in Philadelphia — gives the quarterback breathing room. Against a Bills defense that ranks 30th in EPA per play, with injuries in its secondary, Stafford doesn’t need to be a hero on Monday night. He just needs to take what’s there while his defense controls the game.

Now — Aaron Donald. Think about what happens when you add him to this blitz-heavy scheme. One of the reasons blitzing works is that it creates one-on-one matchups. The offensive line runs out of bodies. But when you have Aaron Donald on the interior, the offensive line is already short on bodies before the blitz even arrives, because teams have to send extra help to him. Even in his comeback this season, by multiple accounts, Donald has kept drawing heavy double-team attention. Now imagine a guard and a center both turning toward Donald, while Shula sends a linebacker or a safety through the gap they just left. That’s the nightmare for an offensive line. Donald doesn’t need to have three sacks to dominate this game. He just needs to occupy two blockers and let the scheme do the rest.

But here’s where it gets complicated, and this is the most important part of the whole video. Josh Allen is not Jalen Hurts.

According to the same film review, Allen ranks ninth in the NFL against the blitz, at 0.11 EPA per dropback. But without the blitz, he ranks third, at 0.28. Read that again. Allen is actually less efficient when you send extra rushers at him. On the surface, that sounds like a green light for Shula to keep blitzing. But there’s a catch. Against man coverage — which is what usually sits behind those blitzes — Allen is excellent, at 0.4 EPA per pass, sixth in the league. Against zone coverage, he drops to 0.12.

So here’s the dilemma. Blitzing makes Allen worse. But man coverage makes Allen better. And the Rams’ aggressive blitzing has been paired with a lot more man coverage. If Shula simply copies the Philadelphia plan — blitz, blitz, blitz with man behind it — Allen could beat it. If Shula goes back to soft zone without pressure, Allen will pick it apart. The answer is probably somewhere in the middle: zone pressures. Send five rushers, drop six into zone behind them, disguise who’s coming and who’s dropping. Make Allen hold the ball for an extra half-second while he figures it out. The film review put it simply: the Rams have to pick their spots and balance man and zone.

That’s exactly where Donald matters. If Donald and Kobie Turner can generate pressure with just four rushers, Shula doesn’t have to blitz as often to get home. He can rush four, drop seven into zone, and still make Allen uncomfortable — attacking the exact situation where Allen’s numbers are weakest. And then, on key downs, he can bring the blitz as a surprise instead of a habit.

And there’s another reason the secondary’s health matters so much here. If Jaylen Watson plays, and if Josh Wallace keeps playing at the level he showed in Philadelphia — where PFF gave him the highest grade on the defense — then Shula can trust his corners to hold up when he does play man coverage. Emmanuel Forbes Jr. was targeted twice against the Eagles and allowed nothing. But if Quentin Lake can’t play, the communication in the back end takes a hit, and disguising coverages gets harder. Every injury on this report affects how aggressive Shula can be.

Now think about Buffalo’s side of this. The Bills’ offensive line is going to see a defense that just tore apart Philadelphia’s protection. And the Bills are coming in banged up — DJ Moore is dealing with a shoulder, and their defense has injury questions in the secondary and on the defensive line. They lost at home to the Patriots, and Josh Allen threw an interception that sealed the game — a throw his own coach said should have been thrown away. A quarterback who’s already pressing to make plays, facing a defense that loves to disguise pressure? That’s a recipe for turnovers. And turnovers win Monday night games.

But let me give Allen his respect, because he’s one of the best quarterbacks in football. He can escape a blitz that gets home and turn it into a 40-yard gain. He can run for first downs when coverage is perfect. He can throw a ball 60 yards on the run. The Rams’ rushers have to stay disciplined in their lanes. A blitz that misses Allen is more dangerous than no blitz at all. That’s why linebacker health matters, too — when Allen scrambles, someone in the middle has to be there to tackle him. Landman getting back to limited practice is encouraging. If he can’t go, that’s another reason to watch for a Kendricks elevation.

So here are my three keys for the Rams’ defense on Monday night. Key one: Donald occupies two blockers on every snap he plays. If Buffalo has to double him, the blitzes get home. Key two: Shula mixes it up. Not a 60 percent blitz rate like Philadelphia — but a smart, disguised plan with zone pressures and some four-man rushes that let Donald and Turner do the work. Key three: discipline. Keep Allen in the pocket, don’t let him escape the edge, and tackle him when he runs. If the Rams do those three things, Buffalo’s offense will have to grind for every point — and the Rams’ offense, with a healthy Puka Nacua and Davante Adams against a banged-up Bills secondary, should be able to take advantage.

And think about the bigger picture. The Rams are already sitting at the top of the Super Bowl odds board after the comeback in Philadelphia, ahead of the Bills. A dominant defensive performance against Josh Allen, in primetime, with the whole country watching, would change the national conversation overnight. Right now, people talk about the Rams’ stars — Stafford, Puka, Davante, Donald, Garrett. After Monday, they could be talking about the Rams’ defense as a unit — and about the coordinator who turned it around in four weeks.

Here’s my bottom line, Rams House. Five weeks ago, people were calling this defense soft. Now it’s a top-three unit by efficiency, it just held Philadelphia to zero-for-twelve on third down, and it’s about to get Aaron Donald back. That transformation happened because Chris Shula was willing to change. Now he faces his biggest test yet: a quarterback who punishes predictable defenses. If Shula can stay aggressive without becoming predictable, Monday night could be the night this defense announces itself to the entire country.
