Two weeks ago I spent a video criticising an offensive decision and assigned it to the offensive coordinator, because that is what the title says. So let me say the thing that’s actually underneath both mistakes, because it’s a principle and I’m going to use it from now on.

The org chart is not the play sheet.

Titles in football are nearly useless for working out who decides things. Men with coordinator in their title sometimes don’t call plays. Men without it sometimes do. Head coaches call offenses. Assistants own entire phases. The job title tells you what somebody was hired to be and tells you almost nothing about which decisions are his.

So the rule I’m giving myself, out loud, so you can hold me to it, is this. I will not assign a decision to a job title again. I’ll ask who called it, and if I don’t know, I’ll say I don’t know.

So here’s tell 30.

The headline converts an admission into a prediction.

And the diagnostic, which I like because it’s a single question you can ask about any sentence.

Which direction in time does the claim point?

Backward-pointing claims can be verified now. Forward-pointing claims can only be deferred. And a title that moves a quote forward in time has taken the one thing in it that could be checked and replaced it with the one thing that can’t.

This is a cousin of tell 20, where a title turned a proposal into a decision, but it isn’t the same animal. Tell 20 changed the certainty of a claim. This one changes its tense. And the tense is where the accountability lives.

Because notice what gets lost. The real sentence is a coach saying he has not been good enough. That is a remarkable thing for a professional to say in public in the middle of a season. The headline version is a coach making bold promises, which is what fans assume coaches do all day and nobody believes.

The title took the most honest sentence in the video and converted it into the least.

And while I’m here, let me refine my own tool, because today’s video showed me where it’s imprecise.

Run the test on Parker’s quote. I got to call plays better. Invert it and you get I should call plays worse, which nobody says. By the letter of yesterday’s rule, that’s an empty sentence.

But it obviously isn’t. So the rule needs sharpening, and here’s the fix.

The inversion test applies to the attribution, not the adverb.

The word better is doing no work. The word I is doing all of it. Because the real alternative to I got to call plays better is not I should call plays worse. It’s they got to execute better. And that sentence is said by coaches at every level of football every single week.

So when you invert a claim, don’t invert the quality word. Invert the subject. Ask who else could have been named, and whether naming them would have been the easier thing to say.

Now the football, and the centrepiece of this video is a piece of data nobody has given me before, and it’s the best-defined problem anybody has put in front of me this season.

Now, before I tell you why that number matters, let me tell you why it’s a better statistic than most of what gets quoted at you.

The opening drive is the single cleanest unit of analysis in football.

Think about what’s true of it and nothing else. Both teams have had a full week. Neither has seen anything the other is doing today. Nobody has adjusted to anything, because there is nothing to adjust to yet. The offense is usually running a scripted set of plays prepared in advance, and the defense is running the plan it spent the week installing.

Which means the opening drive is the only drive in a football game that measures preparation rather than adaptation.

And that’s why five of seven is a serious number. Every other failure in a football game is contaminated. A fourth-quarter collapse might be fatigue, or score effects, or a hurt player, or an offense that stopped giving the defense any rest. An opening-drive touchdown has none of those excuses attached to it. It is the opponent executing a plan against a defense that had seven days to get ready for exactly that.

So I’d put it like this, and it’s the hardest sentence I can write about this defense.

Failing on the opening drive is a preparation failure by definition, because there is nothing else it can be.

And that places it precisely in the ledger. This is not a talent complaint and it is not a hustle complaint. It belongs with the penalty distinction I drew weeks ago, where I split penalties into procedure and discipline. Procedure penalties are an install problem. Discipline penalties are a culture problem. An opening-drive touchdown, five times in seven weeks, is procedure.

Which is, I’ll note, exactly what Parker said. Not in those words. But he went back through the first possession of every game, and he changed how they practice, and he changed how they meet, and he said he has to call plays better. He has independently diagnosed it as an install and preparation problem and he has started fixing it where install and preparation live, which is Thursday and Saturday rather than Sunday.

That is a coach reaching the correct answer by a different route, and it increases my confidence in both of us.

This is the standard I set for myself three weeks ago with asymmetric luck accounting. You do not get to count the evidence that supports you and leave out the evidence that doesn’t. Now, the best question in this entire video, and I want to build it out, because the man asks it and then moves on and it deserves a whole chapter.

He looks at the Philadelphia game and finds three separate failures. Their first touchdown drive took four plays. Their second took thirteen. Then out of halftime, an eighty-four-yard touchdown drive.

And he asks: which one of those are you trying to fix?

That is the right question and almost nobody in football media ever asks it. So let me give you the full version.

Those three drives are three different problems with three different causes and, crucially, three solutions that pull against each other.

Problem one. The four-play touchdown drive. Four plays and eighty-odd yards means somebody got loose. That’s an explosive-play problem, and explosive plays are structural. One blown assignment, one busted communication, one safety who guessed. You fix it by playing deeper, keeping two safeties back, and taking away the top of the defense.

Problem two. The thirteen-play touchdown drive. Thirteen plays means nobody got loose and it didn’t matter. That’s death by four yards at a time, and it’s not structural, it’s cumulative. Nobody failed badly. Everybody failed slightly, thirteen times in a row. You fix it by playing tighter, bringing defenders closer to the line, and making the short completion cost something.

And look at what I just described, because this is the whole point.

The cure for problem one is play deeper. The cure for problem two is play tighter. You cannot do both on the same snap with the same eleven men.

So a defense that has both problems is not suffering from one failure with two symptoms. It is being asked to solve two problems whose solutions are opposites, and the coordinator’s actual job is to choose which one he would rather have.

Which is the Fangio argument I made three weeks ago, arriving from the other side. A two-high, light-box defense has chosen to be bad at problem two in order to be good at problem one. The deal it offers you is take the short stuff. And that’s a real choice, not a failure, right up until the opponent takes the short stuff thirteen times in a row and scores anyway.

And the third drive is a different animal again. The eighty-four-yard touchdown out of halftime.

The halftime drive is the adjustment test. Both sides have had twelve minutes and a whiteboard. The offense has seen your plan for two quarters and has fixed what it didn’t like. You have seen their plan and have done the same. So a touchdown on the opening drive of the third quarter is not a preparation failure and it isn’t an execution failure either. It’s an adaptation failure.

Which means the Philadelphia game produced one piece of evidence about structure, one about finishing, and one about adjustment, and anybody who watches it and concludes the defense is bad has learned nothing, because bad isn’t a diagnosis. It’s a feeling with a word attached.

And I’ll give the video full credit for the part that follows, because it’s the difference between watching football and analysing it.

After a four-play drive, everybody remembers the play that hurt. After thirteen plays, I’m asking which snap we could have won to get everybody to the sideline. Maybe it’s a missed tackle on second down that leaves them a yard to go.

That is process analysis instead of highlight analysis, and the reason it matters is that you can act on it. You cannot coach don’t give up an eighty-yard touchdown. You can absolutely coach make this tackle on second down so third down is seven instead of one.

Now let me give you the three explanations that are actually available, because some of them are fixable without spending a pick and some aren’t.

Explanation one. It’s the coverage.

This is the one almost nobody says out loud, and it’s the most likely. A quarterback who has somewhere to throw in two and a half seconds does not get sacked, no matter who is rushing him. Sacks are not purely a pass-rush statistic. They are partly a coverage statistic.

And I can prove I believe this, because I made the inverse argument three weeks ago. I told you a cornerback’s coverage numbers are partly a measure of his pass rush, because a corner covering for four seconds will lose eventually and a corner covering for two won’t.

Those are the same claim. I was happy to use it to defend a cornerback and I failed to use it to explain a sack total. That’s the error, and it’s the same error as the Fangio prediction last week, where I had the right tool on Tuesday and didn’t carry it to Thursday.

So if Dallas’s coverage is surrendering quick completions, six edge rushers will produce eight sacks no matter how good they are, and a seventh will produce nine.

Explanation two. It’s the second rusher, not the first.

And here the video says something that stopped me, because it’s my own argument in plainer language than I’ve managed.

I can give a rusher credit for making the quarterback move. What gets me is watching that quarterback slide a step, set his feet again, and find somebody. If one guy flushes him, I want to see who’s waiting when he tries to escape.

That is the multiplicative principle, said better than I said it.

A flush is worth nothing by itself. A quarterback driven off his spot has been handed a problem, and if nobody is standing where he’s going, he solves it. Which means the thing Dallas may be missing is not a better rusher but a coordinated second rusher arriving at the escape point. And that is a rush-lane and technique problem, taught on a practice field, not bought at a deadline.

Explanation three. It’s snap distribution.

Six edge rushers is a lot of edge rushers. Somebody is playing twenty snaps who should be playing forty, or four men are splitting work that two should own. Rotation keeps people fresh and it also stops anybody from finding a rhythm against a tackle he’s figured out.

Now the interior, because there’s a piece of coaching in this video that is the best thing I’ve read about defensive line play in weeks, and I want to expand it.

Parker names three men he leans on inside. Quinnen Williams. Kenny Clark. And the man rendered as Otito Abania, which arrives in three different spellings in eight minutes and which I still cannot resolve.

Here’s the piece of coaching, and I’m quoting it because the precision is the point.

If the tackle reads the blocking scheme, holds his ground and gets a hand free, the linebacker knows which gap is his. If the tackle gets pushed sideways, suddenly that linebacker has two gaps to worry about and a back who gets to choose.

Read that again, because it contains the entire job description of an interior defensive lineman and it never mentions a tackle for loss.

Let me build it out.

A defensive tackle’s primary job is not to make a play. It is to reduce the number of answers the man behind him has to consider.

If the tackle holds his ground, the gap on one side of him is closed and the linebacker has one gap to fill. He can play fast, because he has one decision. If the tackle gets displaced sideways, the gap he was closing opens, the gap he was heading for is still open, and now the linebacker has two gaps and must wait to see which one the runner picks.

And here’s the hidden cost, which the video names and most people miss. A back who gets to choose.

A running back facing one open gap must take it, and the defense knows where he’s going. A running back facing two gaps gets to make the linebacker commit first. That is worth yards before anybody has run anywhere, because it converts the linebacker from a man filling a hole into a man guessing.

So the general principle, and it’s one of the most useful things I can hand you.

Interior defensive line play is measured in somebody else’s workload.

Which is why the video’s conclusion is exactly right, and why it echoes the argument I made about Quinnen Williams four days ago. You can understand what this man brings to the front without ever looking for his name in the sack column.

Two of us got to that independently, from different evidence, and that’s the second time this week that’s happened. I’ll take it as encouraging rather than suspicious.

Then the video makes an observation I had not thought of once, and it’s the sort of thing that makes me sit up.

In Philadelphia, Dallas’s defense could hear itself, because the crowd there wanted its own offense to hear the cadence. In Dallas, the Cowboys are the ones making noise when Arizona has the ball.

Home-field advantage is a communication disadvantage for your own defense.

Which means a signal-caller at home has a harder job than a signal-caller on the road, and he has to physically gather people close enough to hear him and get them lined up before the opponent snaps it.

Now the back end, quickly, because there are three real watch items in it.

First, the safety rotation. A player rendered as Alicia Clark got more work against Philadelphia, and Parker likes the ground he covers deep and how quickly he gets involved against the run. And the question the video asks is the right one. Can Clark come up and finish a tackle, then get back deep on the next snap, without giving away the coverage early?

Let me name why that’s hard, because it’s the most underrated difficulty in football.

A safety’s pre-snap depth is information. It’s free information, available to the quarterback before anything happens. A safety who creeps forward to help against the run has told the quarterback there’s one deep defender. A safety who stays deep has told him the run has one fewer body in the box.

So the skill isn’t covering ground. It’s delaying disclosure. Being able to do both jobs is worth very little if your alignment announces which one you’re doing on this snap.

Which is the defensive version of a principle I built four days ago about offensive personnel. Tell 27 was the formation that only means one thing, and the reason heavy personnel announces itself is that eleven men is zero-sum.

And here’s the symmetry, which I think is the prettiest idea in today’s script.

A defense wins by making one look mean two things. An offense loses by making one look mean one thing.

Same principle, opposite sides of the ball. Disguise is the whole currency. And it’s why the Overshown idea in this video is a good one.

Put Overshown near the line and make the protection account for him. Maybe he comes. Maybe he drops and takes away the quick throw the quarterback thought he had. Either way, the same look twice makes the offense solve a different problem.

That’s it exactly. The value isn’t the blitz. The value is that the picture is ambiguous, and an ambiguous picture costs the quarterback a fraction of a second, and a fraction of a second is what the entire pass rush is competing for.

Which, incidentally, is a fourth explanation for eight sacks that doesn’t require buying anybody. If a defense shows the same look and it only ever means one thing, the rush is a step behind before it starts.

That’s the bimodal nature of the job. A cornerback’s workload isn’t spread evenly across a game. It’s nothing, nothing, nothing, and then the entire outcome of a possession, on third down, with no warning.

Which means the measurement problem I described three weeks ago is real in both directions. Three reasons a corner isn’t targeted: he’s good, he’s irrelevant to the scheme, or the quarterback never looked. And nine targets removes that confound, which is why last Monday’s line matters. Nine targets, two catches, ten yards.

And remember the contract. He’s on a one-year deal just over three million dollars with no extension expected. So every Sunday from here is simultaneously a football performance and an audition in a negotiation he will conduct against the whole league in February. That’s an unusual incentive structure and it points the same direction as the football, which is the best you can hope for.

Third, Trey McBride, and here the video gives me something about Arizona I didn’t have.

Mike LaFleur’s offense will take a short completion and trust the receiver to get upfield.

That’s Arizona’s offensive coordinator and it’s a philosophy, and knowing it changes what a good third down looks like.

Because the video’s example is exactly right and it’s the thing I taught earlier this month. Third and eight, McBride catches it five yards downfield. If the defender is close enough to wrap him up, that’s a punt and the call worked. If the defender arrives on time and lets him turn upfield for three more, that’s a first down and the call still worked.

Same coverage. Same throw. Same defender in the right place. Opposite outcomes.

So I’ll put it the way I put it yesterday, because it keeps proving itself. A five-yard throw that becomes a five-yard gain is a defense working. A five-yard throw that becomes a twenty-yard gain is a tackling failure wearing a coverage costume.

The call gave you a chance. Somebody still has to finish it. That’s the video’s line and I can’t improve on it.

And the video asks you a question at the end, so I’ll answer it before I ask you anything.

It asks what would win you over faster on Sunday. An opening three-and-out, or a sack on third and long.

My answer is the three-and-out, and not because it’s prettier.

A sack on third and long is one play. It could be a tackle slipping, a rusher guessing right, a quarterback holding it a beat too long. It’s an outcome, and single outcomes are nearly useless as evidence.

The opening three-and-out tests a claim. A coach told us he changed the preparation for exactly that drive. The drive comes with no in-game information, against a scripted plan, in a stadium where his own crowd makes his defense harder to run. If Dallas gets off the field on that drive, the thing that was changed is the thing that produced the result, and that is as close to a controlled experiment as football ever gives you.

And if Arizona scores on it, that’s five of eight, and the preparation argument gets a lot harder to make.
